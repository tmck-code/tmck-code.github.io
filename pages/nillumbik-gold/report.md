# Gold in the Nillumbik district and surrounding goldfields (Plenty River to Yarra Glen, Kinglake to the Yarra at Kew)

**A cited research report compiled from 1,162 verified claims (round 1: #1–#159; round 2: #160–#371; round 3a, maps and videos: #372–#451; round 3b, heritage records, Trove newspapers, local histories, the Warrandyte Diary and four more videos: #452–#738; round 4, the widened area from the Plenty River to Yarra Glen and Kinglake to Kew: #739–#1162).**

Study area: Nillumbik Shire and immediate surrounds, Victoria, Australia. Centre approx -37.70, 145.18. Localities covered: Warrandyte and North Warrandyte (Anderson's Creek, Fourth Hill, Pound Bend, Whipstick Gully, Black Flat), Diamond Creek, Research and Eltham, Kangaroo Ground, St Andrews (Queenstown), Smiths Gully, Panton Hill (Kingstown), One Tree Hill and Yow Yow (Caledonia diggings), Yarrambat, Hurstbridge and the Plenty River, Christmas Hills. Round 4 widened the area to Templestowe, Bulleen and Heidelberg on the lower Yarra, Eltham, Greensborough, South Morang and Mernda, Arthurs Creek, Kinglake, Steels Creek, Dixons Creek and Yarra Glen, Ringwood, Wonga Park and Croydon (sections 1.5 and 3.12 to 3.23).

Claim ids are cited inline as [#id]. Sources are listed by number and URL in section 8. Where a claim's checker recorded a correction, the corrected wording is used and the note "correction" is added.

## 1. Executive summary

### 1.1 Where gold is most likely to be found, ranked

The evidence supports the following ranking. The ranking combines proven production, geological control, what is known to remain, and legal access. Round 2 evidence changed the order: the Caledonia reefs move up, and the Yarra riverbed moves down.

**1. Warrandyte: Fourth Hill, Anderson's Creek, Stoney Creek, Jumping Creek and the Main South Anticline reef line (highest likelihood, legal access exists).**

- Proven field. Official quartz returns from 1861 to mid-1910 were about 22,275 oz from about 21,044 tons [#123]. Jutson treats this as a minimum. Alluvial returns were never recorded [#123]. eMelbourne calls the field "never a rich goldfield", but the first in the colony where gold licences were issued and the first to have an officially appointed gold commissioner [#256].
- Gold is shallow. The great bulk of recorded gold to 1898 came from within 150 ft of the surface, mostly within 100 ft [#130]. From the 1860s to the early 1880s most reefs were worked down to the water table and abandoned [#219]. Deeper sulphide-zone ore did pay at the Caledonia mine [#130, correction].
- Structural control is known. The main reef line sits in the eastern leg of the Main South Anticline, very close to its axis [#135]. The Great Southern Mine ran NNE parallel to the Main South Anticline [#358]. Reefs strike N10E to N25E, parallel to fold axes, and are mostly thin (under 1 ft) and near vertical [#134]. Reef lengths appear to match the lengths of the folds that contain them [#137].
- A 100 oz nugget, Warrandyte's largest, was found in 1874 [#237]. The 1877 diorite dyke boom produced yields up to 8 oz/ton at the Pig Tail reef [#220].
- Mining continued until 1965 (Monument Mine lease voided 17 November 1965 [#82]; Morton's lease shafts closed 1965 [#249]). The field was never written off.
- Legal access: Parks Victoria now permits prospecting in the beds of the Andersons, Stoney and Jumping Creeks in Warrandyte State Park, upstream of the Jumping Creek Road bridge [#323]. This is wider than the single Fourth Hill section recorded in round 1 [#55, #56, #57]. A Miner's Right is required and mechanical tools are prohibited [#324]. Warrandyte State Park is the only park in the core study area on the Resources Victoria list of parks with designated prospecting areas [#231, correction].
- Stoney Creek, which runs into Pound Bend, had been worked for alluvial gold by 1860 [#177]. Its bed is now a permitted area [#323]. This links a documented historical working to a legal modern site.
- RACV warns that finds at Warrandyte are usually small [#346].

**2. Diamond Creek: the Union–Diamond Creek–Allendale reef line (Mine Hill).**

- The richest single mine in the whole study area. Between July 1907 and June 1914 the Diamond Creek mine yielded 29,833 oz of gold from 23,163 tons of quartz, making it the Caledonia goldfield's most productive mine [#191]. This exceeds the whole Warrandyte field's official quartz return for 1861–1910 [#123].
- Earlier, an adit near the Union main shaft is said to have yielded £60,000 worth of gold from one shoot between surface and adit level [#204, correction].
- The reef is a felspathic dyke resembling the diorites of Woods Point and Walhalla, with a quartz vein on each wall [#204, correction]. In 1905 the Union line was traced about 3/4 mile south across the alluvial flat, with payable stone of 2 to 3 oz/ton said to have been found but flooded by surface water. The Allendale mine, nearly a mile further south, was thought to be on the same line [#205].
- The mine was lost to fire on 20 January 1915 [#33, #191], not to exhaustion. The 1950s reopening found the 1,000 ft shaft flooded to about 350 ft; the company pumped it dry for inspection, then gave up [#171].
- Access: the whole reef lies on private property [#205]. As of 2013 Diamond Creek had no visible signs of its mining past [#172]. The Union site is overgrown, dangerous and may carry cyanide contamination [#207]. No legal prospecting access exists.

**3. Caledonia Goldfield reefs: Oram's Reef (Panton Hill), One Tree Hill, Yow Yow.**

- Junner (1914) states that the Caledonia field, mainly the One Tree Hill, Panton Hills and Yow Yow reefs, yielded more gold than the better-known Warrandyte reefs [#317, correction]. Nearly 25,000 oz came from quartz reefs up to 1890 (Panton Hills 9,000 oz, One Tree Hill 7,500 oz, Yow Yow 7,500 oz), worth nearly £100,000 at £4/oz, excluding alluvial [#279, correction]. About 2,000 oz more came in 1890–1912 [#280].
- Oram's Reef at Panton Hill averaged about three ounces to the ton from 1859 to about 1885, "a record equalled on very few fields". Yields at the greatest depth were richer than in the upper workings. It is the only reef on the field worked below 300 ft [#302, correction]. The 300 ft level alone produced about 2,000 oz [#289].
- One Tree Hill's Swedish Reef gave spectacular specimen yields in 1859–60 [#185, #305] and nuggets of 103 oz (Cobbler's Gully) and 56 oz (Fern Tree Gully) came from its gullies [#296, #314].
- Junner saw no geological evidence that gold stops at depth. Few workings went below the water table [#298, #299]. He suggested small companies could work some mines at a profit [#301].
- The field revived in 1939–1950 (Golden Crown, Black Cameron, Big Ben) [#193, #194] and the Black Cameron mine at Smiths Gully was still an active mining operation in 1999, holding the last in situ operating quartz battery in the St Andrews district [#198].
- Access: no legal prospecting area is confirmed. Much of the ground is private. One Tree Hill and Rifle Range Reserve are under deer-control closures in 2026–27 [#336]. The Blue House Road remains at Panton Hill are heritage protected to a 20 m radius [#229].

**4. Warrandyte: the Yarra River bed (Pound Bend and Thompson's Bend / The Island).**

- Two river diversions were made to work the bed for alluvial gold: The Island, Thompson Bend, from December 1859 [#74, #144, #217] and the Pound Bend tunnel in 1870 [#14, #22].
- The Pound Bend scheme exposed about five kilometres of old riverbed [#15, #22], but the company had to remove up to 14 m of silt before sluicing [#151] and was wound up in September 1872 without paying a dividend [#17, #23, #152]. Bannear nonetheless describes it as probably the earliest and most successful of at least three such tunnels on the Yarra [#211].
- Normal river sluicing used cofferdams ("paddocks") pumped dry by small waterwheels [#218]. A coffer dam built in 1856 behind the Old Post Office still shows as stakes at low water [#161, #240].
- Modern access is restricted. The tunnel is heritage-listed [#162] and the Yarra bed is not in the permitted creek list [#323].

**5. Yarrambat, the Plenty River and Hurstbridge.**

- Hundreds of Chinese miners on the Plenty River banks in the late 1800s [#50, #112]. Smugglers Gully has about 1 km of shallow alluvial workings with at least five hut sites [#210].
- The 1939–47 productive period began with a new reef found outcropping in an orchard at Yarrambat [#193]. Four reef mines operated: Golden Crown, Golden King, Golden Stairs, Golden Gate [#51]. The Golden Crown operated until 1950 [#193]; the council says the Golden King closed in 1984 [#113].
- Hurstbridge has gold adits at Wilton Vale and the 70 m Red Crab Mine tunnel [#208], and a Three Chums mine reported in 1938 [#227].
- Smugglers Gully is in Plenty Gorge Park, where prospecting is not confirmed as permitted.

**6. Research / Swipers Gully / Stoney Creek.**

- Gold found 1855–56, re-searched 1861 [#43, #44, #110]. By 1860 Swipers Gully (Research), Stoney Creek and Dry Creek had all been worked for alluvial gold [#177, correction]. Research was one of the first goldmining settlements of the Caledonia field [#321]. No production figures survive. Now largely residential, except that the Stoney Creek bed within Warrandyte State Park is a permitted prospecting area [#323].

**7. Kangaroo Ground.**

- Small quantities of gold were found at Scotts Hill, Kangaroo Grounds, in 1851 [#174]. Gold was found in 1854 in the Stringy-bark Forest a few miles east of Kangaroo Ground [#270]. Kenny published "Kangaroo Grounds—Alluvial Gold" in 1936 [#227]. One Warrandyte dyke may run north-west to Kangaroo Ground [#133]. Kangaroo Ground prospered in the 1850s as a settlement on the road to the Caledonia diggings [#356]. Minor and indirect; no mine or production figure.

**8. Christmas Hills, Eltham.**

- The One Tree Hill goldfield (1859) is recorded under Christmas Hills by Wikipedia [#353–#355]. It is the same One Tree Hill as the Caledonia field's richest alluvial and reef centre (see 3.6 and 3.8). Christmas Hills was also a route with 13 hotels [#115]. Eltham grew as a commercial centre for miners passing through [#322] and gained a courthouse and police residence in 1859–60 [#116]. No mine is documented at Eltham.

### 1.2 Why the ranking holds

- Warrandyte is first because it combines a long production record (1851–1965), the only published structural geology for the township (Jutson 1910), a clear predictive rule (reefs track fold lengths), and the only legal public prospecting areas in the study area (three creek beds) [#323].
- Diamond Creek is second on remaining ore. Its mine produced more gold in seven years than Warrandyte did in fifty [#191, #123]. Payable stone was found along strike to the south but flooded [#205]. It is blocked by private tenure.
- The Caledonia reefs rise to third. Junner's production figures show they out-produced Warrandyte's reefs [#317, correction], Oram's Reef got richer with depth [#302, correction], and the field was never tested below the water table [#298].
- The Yarra riverbed drops to fourth because both diversion schemes failed to pay [#17, #217] and access is closed.
- Yarrambat has the most recent closures but thin documentation.
- Round 3 evidence (section 1.4 and sections 3.9 to 3.11) does not change the order. It adds the historical prospecting advice of 1865, 1893 and 1903, which names untested ground, and Jutson's 1911 negative conclusion, which closes most of the Warrandyte fold belt [#546, #547, #517, #522, #611]. It also adds Kinglake, Steels Creek and Toolangi, which are at or beyond the edge of the study area and are not ranked.

### 1.3 Access and legal constraints (summary)

- Victoria requires a Miner's Right for any recreational prospecting [#55, #328]. It costs $26 from Services Victoria [#338]. Prospecting without one can attract penalties of up to $36,984 under the Mineral Resources (Sustainable Development) Act 1990 [#339].
- Permitted areas in the study area: the beds of the Andersons, Stoney and Jumping Creeks in Warrandyte State Park [#323]. Hand tools only [#56, #324]. Backfill holes and restore leaf litter [#57, #332].
- Prospecting is banned on prohibited Crown land, which includes parks under the National Parks Act 1975 except designated areas [#232, correction]. Private land needs the owner's or occupier's consent [#233].
- Pound Bend Tunnel is on the Victorian Heritage Register (H1260), registered 19 December 1996, the only gold-related place among the nine VHR entries for Nillumbik Shire [#162, #75, #78, #156]. Disturbing a VHR place or any historical archaeological site is an offence under the Heritage Act 2017. A Miner's Right gives no rights under the Heritage Act [#330].
- Geraghty's Mine has been gated since 2002 [#86]. The former mines in Warrandyte State Park are closed to the public; a 2019 parliamentary question asked whether they would reopen [#268].
- Deer-control night closures apply to Pound Bend Reserve, The Common, Jumping Creek Reserve and Mt Lofty Reserve [#61], and to One Tree Hill, Rifle Range Reserve and the Kinglake Biolink [#336], 13 July 2026 to 30 June 2027.
- Everything else is private land, council reserve or park without a confirmed prospecting permission.

### 1.4 Historical prospecting advice (1865–1911)

Four historical authorities said where to look. They are listed in date order. The 1865, 1893 and 1903 advice is positive. The 1911 advice is negative.

**Warden J. A. Panton, November 1865 [#536–#552].**

- Anderson's Creek was almost deserted. Its workings were shallow alluvial gullies [#537]. Only one reef was still held [#539]. A few miners had found a rich but very limited lead of cement, and the river bed was worked in summer with cofferdams [#538].
- Caledonia was the richer field. Nuggets of 20 oz and 80 oz had recently come from old workings [#540]. A One Tree Hill crushing gave over 40 oz/ton [#541]. The reefs were thin and less continuous than at Bendigo, except those in diorite [#544]. At Yow Yow a vein in syenite (diorite) resembled the Woods Point reefs [#543].
- Advice: try Mullum Mullum Creek, south-south-west of Anderson's Creek, where Silurian rock lies under thin sand with heath and stringybark [#546]. Try the Watsons Creek flats near the Fern Tree Creek junction, the line south along the east of Kangaroo Ground to the Yarra at Anderson's Creek, and the heads of Bell's and Armstrong's Creeks [#547]. The Anderson's Creek reef strikes toward One Tree Hill, so the ranges between them should be prospected for reefs [#548]. The slate spur north of the Yow Yow syenite hill should carry rich veins, because the gullies draining it were rich [#545]. Selwyn had said the subdivision showed good indications over a large untouched area [#549].

**Government Geologist R. A. F. Murray, October 1893 [#515–#518].**

- The gold workings in the Silurian tract between Melbourne and the Goulburn included Ringwood, Templestowe, Eltham, Warrandyte, Panton Hill, Queenstown and the Plenty River [#515]. Quartz had been found and partly worked, with rich results, in many places, but the work was very superficial [#516]. The area between Warrandyte, Panton Hill and the Plenty was "a tempting and easily accessible field" with wood and water [#517].
- Rule: look for reefs where alluvial gullies or leads begin, or where they become richer when crossing certain lines [#518]. Murray's 1894 report noted the anticlinal fold along which the main Warrandyte discoveries lay [#584].

**The Argus, June 1903 [#519–#532].**

- A correspondent described a highly auriferous district from Anderson's Creek to Morang, within 40 miles of Melbourne, in which no organised effort had been made to test the deposits [#519]. Apart from the old Union, no shaft was 300 ft deep [#521].
- Four quartz belts: (1) Anderson's Creek, One Tree Hill and Queenstown; (2) Diamond Creek (Allandale and Union), Arthur's Creek (Norton's) and Upper Diamond Creek (Gardner's); (3) the Pioneer line; (4) Gray's reef at Diamond Creek, Beinke's at Tanck's Corner and Atkinson's at Morang [#522].
- Evidence offered: the Clean Sweep at Arthur's Creek, over 100 tons at 16.5 oz/ton and a 12-ton crushing for 367 oz [#523, #524]; Beinke's find at Tanck's Corner, 15 dwt from two buckets [#526]; Plunkett's Long Gully reef at Panton Hill, about £2,000 [#529]; the Union's reported £70,000 in its last two years (low confidence) [#520]. Verdict: virgin ground apart from surface work, neglected because it was close to Melbourne [#532].

**J. T. Jutson, 1910 (published 1911) [#588–#614].**

- Reefs strike N10°E to N25°E, close to the fold axes, and are near vertical [#588]. Most are 3 in to 2 ft thick, mostly under 1 ft, and workings rarely exceed 200 ft [#589]. The main line follows the Main South Anticline from the Great Southern through Fourth, Third, Second and First Hills to the north bank just west of the Caledonia [#590]. The Main North Anticline has no reefs south of the river [#591].
- Gold to 1898 came mostly from within 100 to 150 ft, in the oxidised zone [#614]. At the Caledonia the rich gold lay above a bedded vein at about 300 ft and practically ceased below it [#607]. The best alluvial returns came from the Yarra bed, not from the gullies [#608].
- Conclusion: the structure of the country does not support any hope of a substantial extension of the field or of a new goldfield [#611]. Reefs outside the Warrandyte folds, including Nillumbik parish, are thin and irregular [#613].
- One opening: gold in the gullies north of the Yarra may have come from isolated reefs, from the dykes, or from gravels under the Kangaroo Ground basalt [#612]. The sub-basalt gravels have not been tested.

**What this means.** Panton, Murray and the 1903 writer describe an untested district. Jutson describes a tested one. The places named in 1865 and 1903 that later work did not close off are Mullum Mullum Creek, the Watsons Creek flats, the eastern Kangaroo Ground line, the Arthur's Creek and Tanck's Corner belt, and the ground under the Kangaroo Ground basalt. Most of that ground is private, and none of it is a designated prospecting area (section 5).

### 1.5 The widened study area (round 4)

Round 4 (claims #739–#1162) widened the search from the Nillumbik core to the surrounding goldfields: the Plenty River from Templestowe and Heidelberg north to South Morang and Mernda, Eltham and Research, Arthurs Creek and Hurstbridge, Kinglake, Steels Creek, Dixons Creek and Yarra Glen, Ringwood, Wonga Park and Croydon, and the Yarra at Kew. The new claims come mainly from Trove newspapers, local histories, heritage records and geological reports. The page now holds 1,162 claims, 481 sources and 62 killed (rejected) claims.

- Templestowe and Bulleen had a real, short-lived reef field. Quartz reefs on the Carlton estate and at Woodfield were opened in 1858, and a 30 oz/ton reef about a mile west of Templestowe was later lost [#1081, #1086, #1104, #1136, #1156]. The Antimony Hill gold and antimony mine ran from 1889 to about 1893 and Antimony Hill miners drowned in August 1892 [#1118, #1119, #1124].
- The Plenty River has the earliest records: a "Plenty Ranges" gold report in May and June 1851, before the Anderson's Creek discovery was announced [#740, #744, #1061]. A deep lead under basalt was worked at South Morang in 1887–1897 [#1115, #1126, #1148].
- Yarrambat and Tanck's Corner, Arthurs Creek and Hurstbridge produced gold in the 1890s–1940s: Golden Stairs at Tanck's Corner (1924–1929), the Yarrambat reef rush of 1939 and Arthur's Creek crushings of 1897–1925 [#816, #817, #841, #849, #780, #787, #797, #819].
- Kinglake, Steels Creek and Yarra Glen have documented rushes in 1861, 1883 and 1892–1898, including the Hunt Bros reef of 1883 and the Lovie reef [#876, #904, #918, #1018].
- Ringwood was an antimony mine that also won gold in 1883 [#1000, #1007, #1009]. Wonga Park has only unemployment-era prospecting records of 1931–1932 [#1046, #1047].
- Negative evidence is also recorded. Government Geologist Murray found no geological indication of a payable discovery between Government House and Heidelberg in 1893 [#1129]. A 15 ft shaft at Studley Park, Kew, found no gold [#1101]. Section 3.23 lists the places where no records were found.
- Diamond Creek remains the best-documented lode field: its mine crushed 3274 tons for 1459 oz in the first quarter of 1870 [#756]. Arthur's Creek took about 20,000 pounds of gold from five shafts by 1906 [#797].

## 2. Geological setting

Three geological sources are in the claim set. J. T. Jutson's 1910 paper covers Warrandyte [#122]. N. R. Junner's 1914 paper covers the Queenstown (St Andrews) area, with a reference back to his 1912 Diamond Creek paper [#312, correction]. David Bannear's 1999 heritage survey quotes earlier Geological Survey reports by Dunn (1905), Whitelaw (1898, 1919), Howitt (1914) and Kenny (1932–1938) [#204, #227]. Two source labels are wrong. The Jutson claims (#122–#137) are labelled "Forbes, 1898" [#122, correction]. The Junner claims (#279–#318) are labelled "Junner 1913, Diamond Creek" but are from the 1914 Queenstown paper, "The Petrology and Mining Geology of the Country near Queenstown", Proc. Roy. Soc. Victoria 27 (N.S.), read 9 July 1914 [#312, correction]. See section 7.1.

**Regional setting.**

- The Victorian gold province is "one of the richest orogenic gold provinces in the world" [#319]. Willman et al. (2010) link variations in gold deposit age and style between the Stawell, Bendigo and Melbourne zones to differences in crustal structure. Warrandyte and Nillumbik lie in the Melbourne Zone. The paper does not mention them, so local application is an inference [#320].
- Dykes of quartz porphyry, felsite and diorite east of Melbourne are often altered and cut by auriferous quartz veins that almost invariably contain stibnite (antimony sulphide) [#311, correction]. Auriferous dykes are known at Diamond Creek, Warrandyte, Templestowe, Kinglake, Frankston and Ringwood [#312, correction].

**Host rocks (Warrandyte, Jutson).**

- Silurian shales and sandstones, with fossiliferous grits and conglomerates [#136].
- The rocks are folded. Named structures: the Warrandyte Anticline (with minor folds on its crown), the Main South Anticline, and the Caledonia (Caledonian) Syncline [#135, #136].
- Junner adds that the Silurian beds near Hurstbridge resemble those at Diamond Creek. The Templestowe anticline, "so marked a feature of Diamond Creek", is visible in a railway cutting just south of Hurstbridge station. The strata dip east at about 45 degrees from there to about 1.25 miles beyond Cottles Bridge, where the Bulleen syncline crosses [#313, correction].

**Reefs (Warrandyte).**

- Quartz reefs strike roughly N10E to N25E, approximately parallel to the fold axial lines [#134].
- Reef thickness ranges from about 3 inches to 2 feet; most are under a foot [#134, correction]. Dip is generally close to vertical [#134].
- The main Warrandyte line lies mostly in the eastern leg of the Main South Anticline, very close to its axis [#135]. The Caledonia reef lies practically along the axis of the Caledonia Syncline [#135, correction]. The Bendigo line placement is uncertain [#135, corrections].
- Reefs are linked to the minor folds on the crown of the Warrandyte Anticline [#136]. Reef lengths appear to correspond to fold lengths [#137].
- The reefs lie on a line of hills: 1st Hill (above the main street), 2nd Hill (Geraghty's), 3rd Hill (Masterton's) and 4th Hill (Richmond's) [#214, correction].
- Junner notes the One Tree Hill reefs dip steeply and cut across the strata at depth, as the Warrandyte reefs do [#317, correction]. Native lead has been recorded from Warrandyte quartz reefs and from the Homeward Bound reef at One Tree Hill [#284, #317].

**Dykes (Warrandyte).**

- Two auriferous dykes lie east of the Caledonia mine. Each is about 8 to 10 ft wide, dipping east at about 60 and 45 degrees. The rock is decomposed and carries thin auriferous quartz veins [#131, correction].
- The dykes were discovered on the north bank of the Yarra in 1877 and caused a small boom (First Hill, Pig Tail, Fourth Hill, Crown and Northern companies) [#220]. They were worked until 1886, to 200 ft [#129]. The heritage timeline dates the Black Flat dyke discovery to 1878, with the Elliot Freehold Company and a stamping crusher [#241], and the Crown Company mine to 1879 [#242].
- A basic dyke at the Caledonia mine was identified as monchiquite [#132].
- According to Whitelaw's map, one dyke runs north-west as far as Kangaroo Ground [#133].

**Depth of gold (Warrandyte).**

- Up to 1898, the great bulk of gold was at and above 150 ft, mostly within 100 ft [#130]. Most reefs were worked to the water table and abandoned [#130, #219].
- From about 1898 the Caledonia mine took gold from the sulphide zone below the water table [#130, correction]. Its main shaft reached 620 ft [#222]. Most gold came from surface to 300 ft [#124].
- Groundwater was also the limit elsewhere. The Pig Tail reef was cut off by a slide at about water level [#220]. At Oram's Reef the ground water level was about 200 ft [#289].

**Diamond Creek (Dunn 1905, via Bannear).**

- The Union lode is a felspathic dyke resembling the "so-called diorites" of Woods Point and Walhalla, with one quartz vein on the hanging-wall side and one on the foot-wall side [#204, correction].
- The Union line was traced about 3/4 mile south across the alluvial flat. The Allendale mine, nearly a mile further south, was thought to be on the same line. The Union shaft at 500 ft was the deepest; other shafts were not worked below 300 ft [#205].
- Junner described arsenopyrite with cruciform twinning from the "Diamond Creek Dyke" and said it is abundant near the vein fissures [#312, correction].
- The Diamond Reef lies on the east side of Diamond Creek, found on private land [#95]. The name "Diamond Creek" comes from crystalline stones on the creek bed [#97].

**Caledonia field (Junner 1914, Bannear 1999).**

- Bannear defines the Caledonia Goldfield as a hilly tract from the Yarra River north across Kangaroo Grounds to above St Andrews (Queenstown), covering all country between Diamond Creek and Watsons Creek. Listed localities include Queenstown, One Tree Hill, Kingston (Panton Hill), Kangaroo Grounds, You You Hill, South Morang, Arthurs Creek, Nillumbik (Diamond Creek), Steeles Creek, Yarrambat and Kinglake [#173].
- *One Tree Hill.* Reefs lie in Silurian brown and white sandstones, some indurated to quartzite. The Buck reef meets a porphyry intrusion [#281]. From east to west the reefs are Buck, Moonlight, Swedish and Homeward Bound, with the Victoria reef further east. They strike about N15–20E and dip steeply, often near vertical [#282]. The hill is part of the divide between Watsons Creek and Diamond Creek; gullies trending NW and S carried alluvial gold with some nuggets, unusual for goldfields east of Melbourne [#186]. An 1895 Queenstown parish plan shows a diorite dyke at the hill. Junner thought the brecciated quartz porphyry was more likely an intrusive pipe; a tunnel from Fern Tree Gully cuts it and the Buck and Swedish reefs [#318, correction].
- *Swedish reef.* Strikes N25–30E, dips SW at about 60 degrees. Most shafts under 100 ft, practically none deeper than 200 ft [#285, #304, correction]. Gold may be localised at intersections with flat pinkish quartz "indicators" [#300].
- *Buck reef.* About 3 ft wide, up to 6 ft, milky quartz at the contact of the One Tree Hill porphyry and Silurian sandstone, entered by a tunnel from Fern Tree Gully. Sulphide mostly pyrite with little stibnite [#283, #307, correction].
- *Oram's Reef, Panton Hill.* Strikes about N25E, dips west. Laminated white quartz 2 inches to 3 ft wide [#287]. Developed to roughly 300 ft or more and about half a mile along strike [#288].
- *Yow Yow.* Gold veins occur within a quartz diorite intrusion and in fractured adjoining sediments; some veins are payable for some distance from the intrusion [#290]. Reefs strike north to north-east and dip steeply [#292]. Stibnite is the main sulphide, with some pyrite and mispickel (arsenopyrite). Gold is fine, worth over £4 per oz, and calcite is a common gangue mineral [#293]. An aplite dyke at the Caledonia Mine (Yow Yow) shows visible stibnite and pyrite [#311].
- Junner saw no geological evidence that gold stops at depth. He thought secondary enrichment uncertain but probably not major, since the reefs are thin and dense [#299].

**Early technology limit.**

- Gold-bearing quartz was found at Warrandyte in the 1850s, but there was no efficient method of crushing it [#68]. On the Caledonia field, ore was carted 30 miles to Langlands' Foundry in Melbourne at about £3 per ton for crushing plus similar cartage, so even ore of about 3 oz/ton did not pay. The field's first crusher was built at Back Creek in February 1856 [#183]. The locality of Back Creek is uncertain; Bannear lists "Back (Diamond) Creek" among the 1855 gullies [#175, #183, correction].

## 3. Goldfield by goldfield

### 3.1 Warrandyte / Anderson's Creek (including Fourth Hill, Whipstick Gully, Pound Bend, Black Flat)

**Before gold.**

- James Anderson set up a cattle station in 1839 just west of present Warrandyte [#1]. He sold it by the mid-1840s to Major Newman [#63]. James Dawson established a second station in 1841 [#62].
- Gold finds are "said to have been made from as early as 1841 but were not reported" [#141, #213, correction]. Hearsay. See section 6.

**Discovery, 1851.**

- Melbourne's Gold Committee offered a 200-guinea reward [#64]. Before this, 1840s prospectors had found small amounts in isolated pockets of Victoria and NSW [#252].
- Louis Michel and William Habberlin found gold on the banks of Anderson's Creek at Fourth Hill in late June 1851 [#2]. Bannear says Michel's party found the first gold in quartz and then in the gravel banks of Anderson's Creek [#212]. The party also included James Furnival, James Melville, James Headon and Benjamin Greening [#72]. Michel's own account describes 10 small grains [#83]. The Manningham Council brochure says he "uncovered a large nugget" [#252, correction]. Prefer Michel's account.
- Dates differ: end of June (Historical Society [#2, #13]), 13 July (ADB, eMelbourne [#2, correction; #254]), July (Wikipedia [#153]), August (VHD [#7, correction]). eMelbourne notes the rival Clunes claim of 7 July 1851 [#254].
- The Argus of 17 July 1851 reported gold at "Deep Creek on the Yarra, near Major Newman's run" [#65]. The find was officially declared on 16 July 1851 [#138].
- On 6 August 1851 Michel led officials to the site, which he named the Victoria Gold Field; locals called it the Anderson's Creek Field [#3, #140, #255].
- Within days almost 200 miners were working the creek [#4]; about 300 within weeks [#12, #212]. First gold licences were issued at Anderson's Creek in September 1851 [#212]. By December only three diggers remained [#212]; the gullies were "almost depleted" [#142].

**Priority dispute.**

- Warrandyte is widely called Victoria's first officially recognised goldfield [#54, #7, #261]. The Warrandyte Historical Society's Victorian Collections page goes further and says Michel was "the first person to discover gold in Australia" [#278]. That is a local-heritage overstatement [#278, correction].
- Clunes (Esmond, 28 June or 7 July 1851) is the main rival [#6, #66, #138, #254]. In 1854 the Government paid Michel £1,000 [#66, #73].
- A 2026 Eastern Melburnian video article repeats the "one man discovered gold here" framing, without naming Michel, the creek or the date [#260, #261].

**Revival, 1854–1860s.**

- The search was revived in 1854 [#143]. Within two years the district's main gold-bearing areas were found and named. By January 1855 Thompson's, Whipstick, Fiddlers and New Chum gullies were open and Strodes and Bartlett's flats were worked [#213].
- In 1855 two stores, an inn and a tent served about 200 miners [#5]. Most worked alluvial gold [#67].
- A punt operated across the Yarra from 1856 until the bridge was completed in 1863 [#251, correction]. The township was surveyed in 1856 [#257].
- Quartz: a quartz crusher operated by February 1856 (Bannear) [#214; see correction, which treats the date as unverified from the quote]. Moore and Gerraty's (Geraghty's) Fourth Hill tunnel of 1856 was reported as the first substantial quartz mining operation in the St Andrews district. In December 1859 they held a 600 x 200 yard claim; by January 1860 the tunnel was 108 ft [#215, correction]. The Historical Society dates small-partnership quartz mining to 1852 [#146].
- The Caledonian diggings drew miners away in the late 1850s and early 1860s [#69].

**River diversions and river sluicing.**

- A first coffer dam was built across the Yarra in 1856 behind the old Post Office to work the riverbed; stakes are still visible at low water [#161, #240]. Cofferdams ("paddocks") pumped dry by small waterwheels were the normal method of river sluicing [#218].
- December 1859: Clarke & Brown's Yarra Yarra Mining Company began a canal at Thompson's Creek (The Island) with about 25 men. The canal was planned 50 ft wide at the top, 12 ft deep and 14 ft at the bottom. It used an 8-hp steam engine, a Start's patented puddling machine and an inclined tramway lifting wash 30 ft. It suspended in June 1860, restarted in November 1860 with two engines and was defunct by October 1861. Small parties then got "excellent results" until the mid 1860s [#217, #74, #77, #144].
- 8 February 1870: the Evelyn Tunnel Mining Company was formed [#16, #21, #149] to cut the Pound Bend tunnel [#15, #22]. An initial survey had examined the idea in 1859 [#211].
- The tunnel took about three and a half months [#150]. It is 6 m wide and 4 m high [#20, #150]. Length: 145 m (VHD, Wikipedia [#15, #20, #53]) against 196 m (WHS [#150]).
- Up to 14 m of silt had to be removed [#151]. The company never paid a dividend and was wound up in September 1872 [#17, #23, #152]. The heritage timeline records "ceased operations in 1972"; this is probably a typo for 1872 [#247, correction].
- Bannear calls the tunnel one of only thirteen known diversion tunnel or cutting sites in Victoria, and probably the earliest and most successful of at least three on the Yarra (the others being Big Peninsula and the Pipeline tunnel) [#211].
- Later hydro proposals (1884, 1888, 1920s) did not proceed [#18, #19, #24, #25].
- Heritage: VHR H1260, registered 19 December 1996 [#162], HO141, Heritage Inventory H7922-0174 [#75, #78, #156, #76]. A death occurred in November 2004 [#26].
- In 1871 a party erected a flume 50 ft above the Yarra to carry water for sluicing Whipstick Gully; hydraulic sluicing there was minor [#218].

**Crushing plant.**

- 1860: a 4-head battery and Chilian mill, pronounced defective in 1861. 1869: the water-powered crusher at Fourth Hill could not run more than two stamps for lack of water; the Loyal Liberal Co. and Union Co. were awaiting machinery [#216, #219]. In 1875 the only crusher was water-driven and useless when the river flooded [#219].
- Grant's Battery, the first steam-powered crushing battery, started behind the old Post Office in 1868 (timeline) [#239] or 1869 (brochure, which calls it waterwheel-powered and "the first of its kind") [#250]. The two sources conflict on power source and year.
- A water-powered Government Stamping Battery was built in 1897 on the riverbank beside the Yarra Street carpark and the bridge. Timber footings are visible at low water [#160]. It was first waterwheel-driven then by portable steam engine [#221, correction]. William Lewis opened a private water-powered battery on the south bank east of the bridge in 1898 [#245, correction]. The Warrandyte Battery Trust later took over Lewis's battery, and the government battery east of the bridge was dismantled; the timeline gives 1909 and 1925 for these events, unverified [#246, correction].

**Conflicts to note.**

- Caledonia depth: 600 ft (Jutson), 620 ft (Bannear), 187 m (WHS), 180 m (VNPA). Closure: 1909, 1912, 1913 or 1914 [#125, #148, #222, #244, #269].
- Caledonia production: 12,772 oz to 1909 [#124] vs 2,600 oz 1904–08 [#147]. Prefer Jutson.
- Victory closure: 1904 is now supported by three sources [#126, #221, #243]. The 1929 date is the start of sanitary dumping, not mining [#80, #243].
- Grant's battery: 1868 steam [#239] vs 1869 waterwheel [#250].
- Fourth Hill tunnel start: 1856 (Bannear, VHD) [#215, #362] vs June 1859 (walk page) [#84]. Bannear's 1856 date may be the start of Moore and Gerraty's operation, with the 1859–60 dates being the main drive.

**Later history.**

- Around 1900 about 250 miners worked in Warrandyte [#157]. By the 1970s the township was mainly residential, but Pound Bend and Whipstick Gully still drew tourists and weekend gold prospectors [#259].
- Most vertical shafts were filled in by the Mines Department, probably in the 1950s [#248, correction].
- Pre-1939 structures were destroyed in the 1939 fires [#159]. The Miners Hut was probably built in the 1950s [#88].
- The Gold Memorial cairn was unveiled 9 November 1935 [#7–#9, #11] and restored in 1969 [#10]. Parks Victoria gives its location as about -37.749768, 145.218145 [#327].
- The Old Post Office (1876), now the Historical Society museum, survived flooding in 1934, bushfire in 1939 and arson in 1982 [#253]. The Federation Playspace is themed on the mining history with a mine shaft, miner's hut and gold wagon [#366].
- Parks Victoria says relics can be found at Pound Bend, Fourth Hill and Black Flat [#325]. Fourth Hill is disturbed ground with shafts, tunnels and a maze of signposted tracks [#370, correction]. The Fourth Hill Gold Heritage Circuit Walk is 1.5 km, about 1.5 hours [#326, correction].
- On 30 October 2019 the Warrandyte MP asked whether Parks Victoria intended to reopen the former gold mines in the park to the public or run formal tours, citing Historical Society concerns that the mines had been closed [#268].

**Round 3 detail: discovery, 1851 (Long, 1933).**

- C. R. Long wrote in 1933 that Michel's party found gold in quartz on 30 June 1851 near Deep Creek (now Mullum Mullum) and traced the reef some distance [#575]. Michel showed the site to Dr Webb-Richmond on 5 July and wrote to La Trobe the same day [#576]. Commissioner Fenwick visited on 6 August, and every dish of wash-dirt showed gold [#577]. By 20 August 150 to 200 men were working three miles of the creek [#578]. Licences cost 30 shillings a month, the first was numbered 47, and the field was proclaimed on 1 September 1851, before Clunes, Buninyong and Ballarat on 20 September [#579]. Customs recorded 18 oz exported in August 1851 [#581]. The committee's reward was 200 guineas for a workable mine within 200 miles of Melbourne [#580].
- eGold records rumours of gold through the 1840s [#642, #658]. The 30 October 1856 township plan shows the Andersons Creek, Specimen and Whipstick gullies already worked out [#478, #661]. A local oral account of a "tent city" five kilometres long [#708] does not fit the 150 to 200 men of August 1851 [#578, #4]. Low confidence.

**Round 3 detail: the river schemes of 1859 and the Evelyn Tunnel of 1870.**

- November 1859: the Yarra Yarra Sluicing Company fixed on a tongue of land inside the upper end of the bend, and a large body of men was already cutting the channel [#499, #500]. A second company, the Warrandyte and Yarra River Company, chose a site half a mile downstream and held Whipstick Gully [#501], whose dirt was said to yield 1 to 2 dwt per load [#502, #503]. The press called these the first large-scale attempts in Victoria to reach gold in a river bed [#504]. Jutson judged the Thomson's Bend cut a failure because the river broke the dam [#609].
- The Yarra was turned into the Evelyn Tunnel on 23 July 1870 [#571]. The contemporary report gives 639 ft long, 18 ft wide and 14 ft deep [#572]. It was cut through clay slate in three and a half months for £2,100, against an engineers' estimate of £10,000 and twelve months [#573, #574]. 639 ft is about 195 m. This supports the Historical Society's 196 m [#150] and the Doncaster study's "nearly 200 m" [#672] against the 145 m in the heritage records [#15, #20, #496, #722]. See 7.2. The 1859 survey involved pound keeper John Hutchinson [#672]. Jutson treated the scheme as a failure, although patches of gold were spread around the bend [#610]. A water race said to be Chinese-built may survive in the park [#673].
- Coffer dams date from about 1856; Westgarth reported on them in 1857; Tom Logan revived the practice in the 1930s Depression [#676, #697]. Jutson found that the Yarra bed gave the best alluvial returns [#608]. By 1865 the field was almost deserted [#537–#539].

**Round 3 detail: crushing plant, Grant's battery and the Yarra Tunnel.**

- In the 1860s the only machinery was one horse-driven crusher and Wilkinson's calcining furnace [#674], and crushing cost up to £4 a ton [#597]. Lewis Grant set up the first water-powered crusher in the Yarra north of the post office in 1868; only two stampers worked at low river [#463, #675, #663]. Jutson also dates the water-powered plant to 1868 and says it boosted quartz mining [#597]. The 1868 water-powered date now has four sources against the timeline's "steam, 1868" [#239] and the brochure's "1869" [#250].
- In 1869 Grant formed the Yarra Tunnelling Co. to work a claim upstream of The Island. The tunnel ran 70 ft under the river with shafts to both banks. Grant sold to David Mitchell in 1874, and the mine reopened in 1884 and closed in 1888 [#462, #669]. Jutson: the Yarra Tunnel Reef was probably the Caledonia Reef continued under the river. It gave 628 oz 9 dwt from 311 tons, almost all within 100 ft of the surface [#601, #602].
- The 1897 State Battery stood on the bank just east of the Yarra Street bridge; a cairn marks the site [#479, #698]. The Doncaster study gives 1898 [#663].
- Puddling: in 1859–60 there were two puddling machines, McDonald and Cameron's horse-driven one and Dr Owen's steam "Patented Puddling Machine", run by the Yarra Yarra Steam Puddling Co. in Whipstick Gully and closed quickly for lack of gold and drainage [#464, #666]. A circular horse-driven puddling machine survives at Black Flat [#465].

**Round 3 detail: official production and reef geometry (Jutson).**

- 138 tons for 153 oz, October 1861 to June 1868 [#595]; 725 tons for 930 oz, June 1868 to the end of 1870 [#596]. Yields fell in 1881–90, when men left for the land boom [#598]. 1891–1900 almost equalled 1871–80, nearly half of it from the Victory in three years; 1901 to mid 1910 was four times the previous decade, almost wholly from the Caledonia [#599]. Fifty years of quartz and dyke gold was worth under £100,000, with about £51,000 from the Caledonia and £7,066 from the dykes [#600].
- Victory: 1,570 oz from 1,038 tons, September 1896 to September 1899, mostly from 100 to 150 ft; abandoned 1904 [#604]. Pigtail: 387 tons for 1,409 oz in 1875 at 80 ft or less; a slide at 170 ft in 1881; Caledonia Consols retried it from 1905 without payable results [#603].
- Reefs strike N10–25°E [#588] and are thin [#589]. The main line follows the Main South Anticline from the Great Southern through the Fourth, Third, Second and First Hills [#590, #670]. Old Fourth Hill miners said gold came where west-dipping "droppers" met east-dipping bedded veins, and carried for 10 to 30 ft along the bedded vein [#593]. Miners believed the dyke cut off the reefs; Jutson disagreed [#592]. Miners with South African experience likened the Fifth Hill conglomerate to the Witwatersrand banket; it was not payable [#594].

**Round 3 detail: the Caledonia mine, Black Flat.**

- Formerly the Newhaven (about 1903), at the north end of the Caledonia Syncline near the Yarra [#605, #466]. Run by Mr Till from about 1904–05 with up to 250 men [#466, #497]. Officially opened on Wednesday 12 September 1906, when Mrs Fritsch christened the engine "Good Queen Bess" [#564]. The plant was a 10-head battery with 1,050 lb stamps, a concentrating table and two boilers, and cost about £3,500 [#565]. By then 3,075 tons had given 5,208 oz worth £20,910, and £8,625 had been paid in dividends on a paid-up capital of £1,696 [#566]. Minister Cameron said the field had sunk into oblivion after 1855 because people believed the gold "did not live down"; the Caledonia dispelled that idea [#567]. Director Miller credited W. Schlichting of the South Caledonia with persuading him to invest [#568].
- Dividends reached £12,583 15s on called-up capital of £8,250 by 1909 [#606]. A 1905 crushing gave 145 oz from 80 tons [#468, #668]. Returns were good until 1908, when water seepage set in; government help in 1909 failed and the machinery was sold in April 1909 [#468, #469, #686]. The rich gold lay between the surface and about 300 ft, above a bedded vein on an old fault plane; below it gold practically ceased [#607]. Main shaft 620 ft [#467, #686]. The 2006 Manningham citation gives "$102,178" (probably pounds) of gold and £25,166 in dividends on £12,500 capital, about double Jutson's £51,088 [#467, #667, #124]. Prefer Jutson.
- Remains: a concrete machine bed, mullock heaps, and a boiler moved to another private property [#470, #686]. Till became an orchardist on what is now Tills Drive [#471]. A c1907 photograph shows the poppet head [#700]; Jack Till is captioned as manager in another [#704]. Caledonia Consolidated was unwatering the shaft in February 1914 [#557], and Kruse and party restarted the old North Victory lease the same month [#558]. The 2006 park plan says mining largely ceased in 1909 [#495].
- Neighbours: the Black Swan (a group of trees) and the South Caledonia at 308 Yarra Street (a concrete machine bed, workings buried below the street) [#472, #473, #678].

**Round 3 detail: The Dyke, Pigtail, Monument and the Fourth Hill tunnels.**

- The Dyke: the Warrandyte Freehold Gold-mining Company was formed in 1876 to work the diorite dykes on the Elliott Freehold Estate (Captain Selby's after 1883). Nearly half of Warrandyte's gold is said to have come from this property. The tunnel collapsed and is now a deep cleft [#461, #665, #716]. The Crown Company followed the dyke for over 1 km to 40 m depth at about 2.5 g/t. The roof collapsed along nearly the whole length after a shift left, and ore carts and tracks remain buried [#687]. The Black Flat Mining Area (Tills Drive) is of regional significance and holds Warrandyte's last major mine [#460]. The dyke dates (1876, 1877, 1878) and the company name (Warrandyte Freehold or Elliot Freehold) differ; see 7.3.
- Pigtail: started in 1874 by Henry Stiggants senior and junior with Holloway and Lewis Grant; 1,500 oz in two years [#474, #677]. The Pigtail Quartz Mining Co., with Grant as manager, was ruined by a legal dispute. Its opponent Lawler formed a new company that recovered substantial gold before a landslide closed the mine. Caledonia Consuls worked it in 1906 [#475, #677]. Pigtail Track is named after the reef [#715]. Stiggants was still a miner in 1899 [#476].
- Monument: a 15-year lease taken out in 1951 by W. A. R. Manton, F. W. Birdsley and G. Lane (Mines Department lease 9118) in Fidlers Gully on the north side of Andersons Creek, worked to 1965; two shafts and a hut remain [#671]. The Fahey walk text dates the two shafts to 1953–65 and says no gold was found [#680].
- Geraghty's: Patrick Geraghty and William Moore drove 122 m into Fourth Hill in 1859 and missed the reef. Two miners reworked it in the 1880s. A locked steel door is about 12 m inside (15 m per the Diary) and closes off about 800 m of passages [#682, #709]. Beyond the grill are drives left and right, a large stope with miners' foot-holds, and a ventilation shaft; graffiti is one reason it is closed [#683]. The Historical Society runs tours during the Warrandyte Festival [#682]. A 1931 photograph shows children at the open entrance [#701].
- Johnson's: an adit about 60 m straight in and then about 20 m left, on a 2 cm quartz vein with no gold. Parks Victoria fenced it, and it stays open because bats roost in it [#681, #710, #727]. Jackson's Mine is a short adit with a colony of bent-winged bats [#684]. Fifth Hill West is a 30 m tunnel [#678]. The Whipstick Gully battery survives as a brick foundation beside grated shafts; the poppet head is a 1995 reproduction and the quarries date from the 1940s and 50s [#685, #714]. The Fourth Hill Air Shaft is covered and lies about 100 m off Betton Track [#713].
- The Hanson tour: the Hanson family's Historical Goldmine Tour has run since 1978 from the top of Webb Street [#705]. Their 4 ha block, bought in 1969, was the "Westbury" Mining Reserve [#711]. John and Peter Hanson uncovered a shaft and side tunnel at the house in the 1970s [#712]. Visitors can walk about 10 m into the Fourth Hill mine; the other mines were sealed for public liability [#707, #710]. Guide John Hanson says red box, long-leaf box and red stringybark were taken as signs of gold country [#706]. Peter Hanson's 2018 talk named the tunnel site and the capped shafts [#720].
- Miners' houses: the Yarra Gold cottage, where Tom Logan took out his last licence in 1925 [#490, #679]; Russell's slab cottage on his claim at 8 Russell Road [#679]; the 1875 stone schoolhouse, built by the miner-stonemasons Masterton and Sloan [#717]. Anderson's Creek Cemetery is given as 1866 [#718].
- East of the township: the McIntyres Road mine at Park Orchards, possibly the closest mine to Melbourne [#485, #486], and hearsay Wonga Park sites at Hoopers Road, Jumping Creek and Break of Day Gully [#487–#489]. Low confidence.
- Park rules from the 2006 plan: prospecting under Section 32D of the National Parks Act in 6.5 ha of creek bed (1% of the park); hand tools, pans and simple cradles; backfill [#493, #494]. Old shafts and adits at Whipstick Gully, Fourth Hill, Black Flat and the Timber Reserve are hazards [#498].

**What remains.**

- Jutson's returns exclude alluvial gold [#123]. The creek beds and river gravels were never measured.
- Reefs were worked mostly to the water table before 1898 [#130, #219]. Below that only the Caledonia was seriously tested.
- The Great Southern and Sailor's Reef lines on private land are now almost forgotten [#361].
- Access: the beds of Andersons, Stoney and Jumping Creeks [#323, #324].

### 3.2 North Warrandyte

- The Pound Bend Tunnel lies on the Nillumbik/Manningham boundary [#78, correction]. The 1877 diorite dykes were found on the north bank of the Yarra [#220]. The Loyal Liberal Co. worked "north of Warrandyte" in 1869 [#216]. The Yarra Tunnel reef, "on the Yarra north of the island" [#128, correction], may be on the north bank.
- Stoney Creek, which runs into Pound Bend, was worked for alluvial gold by 1860 [#177, correction] and its bed is now a permitted prospecting area [#323].
- eMelbourne lists North Warrandyte among the goldmining settlements established after the 1854 Caledonia discovery [#321].

- Round 3: the Island Diversion Cutting at North Warrandyte is the Yarra Yarra Mining Company canal of 1859–61. Floods have made it the main channel, and the old bend is a willow-choked backwater [#480, #723]. Jutson: the Main North Anticline has no reefs south of the river, and the Loyal Liberal and other reefs become scarce north of it [#591]. Gold in the northern gullies may have come from isolated reefs, the dykes or gravels under the Kangaroo Ground basalt [#612]. A collapsed adit called the "Hidden Mine" has a grated entrance and reportedly a winze [#688]. Sloan's cottage (c1880s) and the Castle Road slab cottage (pre-1904) are miners' houses [#491, #492].

### 3.3 Diamond Creek

**Discovery.**

- Gold mining was active across the district in 1861 [#27]. Gold was discovered at Diamond Creek in the early 1860s [#164]. Joseph and David Wilson found gold in 1862 on land bordering Dr Phipps and Charles Orme [#28, #46]. Victorian Places says the Diamond Reef was discovered in 1863, "resulting in substantial gold mine workings" [#262]. Use "1862–63".
- The first European inhabitants were mostly timber getters and paling splitters; miners came later [#266, correction]. A post office, Methodist church and school followed by 1864 [#264, #96]. The township was surveyed in 1852, subdivided and sold in 1866, and named Nillumbik in 1866–67 [#41, #264, correction].
- W. W. Herbert used mining profits to buy 212 acres in 1866 [#93, #94].
- A new reef called the Pioneer, about 3 miles from Diamond Creek, was found in the mid 1860s. The Pioneer and Union companies worked it in 1870 with substantial plant; one company had a battery at its tunnel end where ore trucks tipped into the hopper. Both stopped by 1875 [#188, correction].

**Mines.**

- The early working was a tunnel on the south side of Mine Hill at the end of Dering Street, mined with a king-sized skip truck [#164, #47, correction]. By 1870 it was the Union goldmine, a major operation with a shaft, steam winder and battery [#165, correction].
- Union Company chronology (Bannear): 1870 new shaft and pumping machinery; 1872 reorganised; December 1875 abandoned; 1903 syndicate; 1904 floated but mining ceased in November; 1906–07 10-head battery; work ceased 1908; 1909 Diamond Creek Co. sank to 450 ft and held the Old Union lease. Two main periods: 1870–75 and 1903–09 [#206]. WikiNorthia: the Union gold ran out in 1875 [#166]; exploration in 1904 found further good ore via a new shaft further east with a winder using wire cables; the mine reached eight levels and closed in 1908, when all machinery was removed [#167, correction]. Before wire cables, worn rope cables caused many accidents [#363, correction].
- Dunn (1905): the Union shaft at 500 ft was the deepest; the last crushing gave 542 oz from 520 tons [#205]. An adit near the main shaft is said to have yielded £60,000 worth of gold from one shoot [#204, correction].
- After the Union closed its boiler was trucked to the Diamond Creek Goldmine on top of Mine Hill [#168]. The Union winder foundations were left overgrown [#168].
- In 1868 a shaft was sunk from the top of the hill (Diamond Creek Mine) [#47]. The Diamond Creek Company planned in 1882 to work Phipps Reef with a 10-head battery and 30-hp engine, but was reported four years later as collapsing "without even striking a pick" [#190].
- The Diamond Creek mine's big engine house contained five boilers powering the winder, battery, generator, air compressors and a massive beam water pump [#169, correction]. The mine supported 200 men [#89, #111]. A 1912 map shows the escape route via the Union [#32].
- Production: between July 1907 and June 1914 the mine yielded 29,833 oz from 23,163 tons, the Caledonia goldfield's most productive mine. Winze workings went to 955 ft [#191]. Howitt reported on the mine in 1914 [#227].
- The Nillumbik Mine, Collard Drive (H7922-0242), was worked 1905–1950 [#91]; Kenny reported on it in 1936 [#227].
- The Eltham–Hurstbridge railway through Diamond Creek opened in 1912 [#267, correction].

**The 1915 fire.**

- On 16 January 1915 the shareholders heard that cleaning the mine had returned 199 oz [#38]. Over six months the co-operative crushed 562 tons for 634 oz [#39].
- At about 3 pm on 20 January 1915 a boiler-room spark ignited a wood heap [#33]. Men at the 900 ft level climbed by disused ladders [#34]. Others found their way to the old Union workings through connecting tunnels, walked out at the Union site, then walked round to the ruins to wait for their mates. No lives were lost [#170, #34].
- Damage was about £3,000; water was rising; about 200 families lost income [#37]. The plant was destroyed [#191]. The Union infrastructure was also damaged [#49].

**After mining.**

- Census: 306 (1871), 187 (1881), 1,127 (1911), 669 (1933). The 1911 figure is the highest of the early series and coincides with the mine's best years [#265, correction; #191].
- Orchards prospered from the mid-1880s to about 1912 [#266, #42].
- In the early 1950s the mine reopened with an electric winder and head frame. The 1,000 ft shaft was flooded to about 350 ft. It was pumped dry for inspection, but progress stopped, the water returned, the head frame and winder were removed, and shareholders were left with worthless certificates [#171].
- The Historical Society believes gravel beaches near the railway are mine waste from the Union outlet [#90].

**Round 3 detail: discovery, the Union and the 1903 survey.**

- The Wilsons' 1862 find was a chance find of gold-bearing quartz near Dr Phipps' and Charles Orme's land [#617]. A church-schoolroom followed in 1862 and a school in December 1864 [#620, #619]. A 1932 article names Peers, Haley and Wilson and gives 1863 [#508, #509]. The Nillumbik Historical Society gives 1862; Victorian Places and the 1996 council video give 1863 [#617, #624, #262].
- The 1932 recollection says the Union opened "nearly 70 years ago" (about 1863–68), with the Diamond Creek Mine some years later [#510]. The shafts were then about 500 ft (Union) and 700 ft plus 300 ft on the underlay (Diamond Creek) [#511]. The Union reportedly banked £70,000 in its last two years (low confidence; a New South Wales "Diamond Creek" company exists and must not be confused with it) [#520]. In 1903 a small party on the old Union surface ground had a last crushing of over 6 oz/ton, and Gray's reef and the Pioneer lease were re-taken up [#531]. In April 1904 the Union was extending a tunnel on a reef with fair gold and unwatering the main shaft [#533].
- The 1903 Argus survey put the Union and Allandale on the second of four quartz belts, with Norton's at Arthur's Creek and Gardner's at Upper Diamond Creek [#522]. The Clean Sweep at Arthur's Creek averaged 16.5 oz/ton over 100 tons, gave 367 oz from 12 tons, and had won just on £10,000 from a 2 ft reef at 40 ft; the reef strikes 12° west of north with shoots pitching south [#523–#525]. Beinke's June 1903 find at Tanck's Corner gave 15 dwt from two buckets, richest in the slate seams; the writer guessed the Smugglers Gully nuggets were shed from it [#526–#528].
- The Diamond Creek Mine before the fire: a 500-ton crushing from No. 7 level (700 ft) gave about 601 oz, reported on 16 January 1915 [#559]. No. 8 level (800 ft) gave 58 oz from 118 tons, and a 6-ton trial from No. 9 level (950 ft) gave about 33 oz from a 12 in reef; a motion to float a larger company was rejected [#560, #561]. The Argus of April 1909 described stopes of reef 6 to 8 in wide and the battery running full time [#534]. The surface plant had four boilers and five steam engines [#622]; the boilers burned logs brought by bullock wagon, and a fourth boiler came from the Union [#618]. Managers recalled were Bass, Alston, Haley and the Christians; three shifts ran at times [#512]. A rumour valued the gold at £1,000,000 to £2,000,000; the 1932 writer called such stories "furphies" [#513].
- The fire: the Maitland Daily Mercury put the damage at about £5,000, with everything on the surface lost except the changing house and office [#562]. Men at the 950 ft level left by the escape shaft after about three hours, because flames in the main shaft stopped them [#563]. The Nillumbik Historical Society gives £3,000 and the 900 ft level [#37, #34]. See 7.2. Fruit growing carried the town afterwards [#625]. The shaft was over 290 m at its 1915 peak [#622]; a photograph caption gives 950 ft [#702]; the 1932 account gives 700 ft vertical plus 300 ft on the underlay [#511]. See 7.2.
- Photographs: Museums Victoria holds two c1905 Albert Jones negatives of the mine, public domain [#623]; Picture Victoria holds a c1890 view with one boiler [#621]. See 8.1.
- The 2019 "Diamond Creek Gold Mines" video shows two unnamed adits near the creek: one with a 30 ft left branch and visible caving, and one running west 80 to 100 ft to a north–south stope with inch-wide quartz veins; mullock heaps and open shafts lie above [#733–#736]. In September 1932 prospectors were again working the gullies [#514].

**What remains.**

- The 1915 fire closed a mine that had just produced nearly 30,000 oz in seven years [#191]. The reef below 900 ft was flooded, not exhausted [#37, #171].
- Payable stone of 2 to 3 oz/ton was said to have been found along strike to the south in 1905, but was flooded by surface water. The whole reef lies on private property [#205].
- The Union site has the oldest and best preserved quartz mining relics in the Caledonia Goldfield, but is overgrown with blackberries, has dangerous workings and a cyanide contamination risk [#207]. As of 2013 the town had no visible signs of its mining past; the two major mines were on Mine Hill behind the Op Shop [#172, correction].

### 3.4 Research and Eltham

- Gold was found in Swipers Gully in 1855–56 [#43]. Mining resumed when the gully was "re-searched" in 1861 [#44, #110]. By 1860 Bannear lists Swipers Gully (Research), Stoney Creek, Dry Creek, Long Gully, Ford's Gully, Spanish Gully, New Watery Gully and Surveyors Gully as worked for alluvial gold; the "by 1860" date and the Fryers Creek entry are unverified [#177, correction].
- Research was one of the goldmining settlements established after the 1854 Caledonia discovery [#321].
- Small-scale mining and firewood cutting continued, then declined [#45].
- Eltham: the 1854 Caledonia discovery encouraged Eltham and Greensborough to grow as commercial centres serving travelling miners; in the 1860s and 1870s many miners selected small blocks of land [#322, correction]. The police residence (1859) and courthouse (1860) followed [#116]. The former Shire of Eltham once extended to the Kinglake district [#357, correction].
- No production figures or named reefs for Research or Eltham survive.

- Round 3, Templestowe: the Templestowe Anticline runs nine miles from Koonung Koonung Creek to north of Nillumbik parish, crossing Diamond Creek south of the township; a gold-and-antimony reef at Templestowe was unpayable [#615]. A reef near Thompsons Road and Feathertop Avenue was mined; little remains [#664]. Panton's 1865 advice to try Mullum Mullum Creek points to the same country [#546]. The Yarra Track to the Woods Point diggings ran from the Diamond Valley in the 1860s [#641].

### 3.5 Kangaroo Ground

- Small quantities of gold were found at Scotts Hill, Kangaroo Grounds, in 1851, but the Caledonia field was not rushed until early 1855 [#174, correction]. This is the earliest find in the Nillumbik hills, in the same year as Anderson's Creek.
- Gold was found in 1854 in the Stringy-bark Forest a few miles east of Kangaroo Ground, which brought substantial changes to the district [#270, correction].
- Kangaroo Ground lay on the road from Melbourne to the Caledonia diggings and prospered in the 1850s [#356, correction].
- Bannear includes Kangaroo Grounds in the Caledonia Goldfield and cites Kenny's 1936 Geological Survey record "Kangaroo Grounds—Alluvial Gold" [#173, #227]. The text of Kenny's record is not in the claim set.
- Jutson reports, from Whitelaw's map, that one Warrandyte dyke runs north-west as far as Kangaroo Ground [#133]. The dykes were low grade [#129].
- No mine or production figure for Kangaroo Ground is documented. The Andrew Ross Museum at Kangaroo Ground holds the diary of Thomas Young (1821–1910), who carted ore between the district's mines and Nickinson's crusher at Panton Hill from June 1865 to July 1866 [#271, #272, correction].

- Round 3: Panton (1865) advised a trial of the Watsons Creek flats, the line along the east of Kangaroo Ground to Anderson's Creek, and the heads of Bell's and Armstrong's Creeks, and said the Anderson's Creek reef strike pointed to One Tree Hill [#547, #548]. Jutson (1910) noted the older basalt at Kangaroo Ground and suggested that gold in the northern gullies may come from gravels under it [#616, #612]. Nobody has tested the sub-basalt gravels.

### 3.6 Caledonia Goldfield: St Andrews (Queenstown), Smiths Gully, Panton Hill, One Tree Hill, Yow Yow

**Extent.**

- Bannear defines the field as the hilly country from the Yarra north across Kangaroo Grounds to above St Andrews, between Diamond Creek and Watsons Creek [#173]. The Caledonia diggings were named after the Caledonia run, which dates from 1841 [#263].

**Discovery.**

- Scotts Hill, Kangaroo Grounds, 1851 (small quantities) [#174]. Caledonia fields, August 1854 [#117]. Streams flowing into Watsons Creek, 1854 (Nillumbik Council) [#275]. Smiths Gully (Bostons Gully), late 1854 [#105]. Victorian Places: "further upstream" on Diamond Creek, 1855 [#263].
- The rush came in early 1855 with a rich strike in Old Caledonia Gully [#174, correction; #121]. Diggers then opened Ironbark Gully, Back (Diamond) Creek, Watsons Creek, New Caledonia Gully, Yow Yow, Wild Dog, Cherry Tree Gully, Happy Valley, Red Shirt Gully and One Tree Hill (Distil Creek). One Tree Hill became the richest alluvial place on the diggings [#175].
- About 3,000 miners by 1855 [#98]. Hundreds of miners made life difficult for the Wurundjeri people [#275].

**Settlements.**

- Market Square, the first village, stood on the east side of Ruggy's Gully about 1.5 miles below St Andrews [#176, correction], where Smiths Gully turns north into Old Caledonian Gully [#106, #118, #119]. Queenstown Cemetery is on the site [#52, #114].
- Queenstown post office 1856, church school 1858, surveyed 1859 [#100]. Panton Hill (Kingstown) was established as a goldfield in 1859 [#349]; a school opened in 1865; it was formally renamed Panton Hill in 1874 after Joseph Anderson Panton, Commissioner for the Anderson's Creek and other goldfields, who mapped the Yarra Valley about 1862 [#351, #352]. By 1880 it had two hotels and a Church of England [#352].
- Smiths Gully school 1867, building 1876 [#104].
- Thomas Young and his wife Brigid raised a large family in a bark-and-slab hut in a bush clearing on the field in 1865–66 [#273, correction]. Mick Woiwod's "Golden Days on the Caledonian Diggings" (Andrew Ross Museum, 2005, 66 pp) draws on his diary [#274, correction].

**Alluvial mining.**

- By 1860 alluvial mining was precarious; Bannear records three men earning enough in Fern-tree Gully in four months to return to England [#179, correction]. In 1860 a party prepared extensive sluicing in Wild Dog Gully with a hydraulic hose and the field's first water race. In 1865 only six puddling machines worked, Whiskey Gully was a favoured puddling location, and a steam puddling machine was erected on Old Caledonian Creek [#180].
- By 1865 alluvial locations also included Hugh Glass's Gully on the Plenty River at South Morang, gullies on Mountain Creek above Mt Slide, and Fern-tree Gully at One Tree Hill [#178].
- Junner: most alluvial gold came from creeks draining One Tree Hill (Cobbler's Gully, Happy Valley, Fern Tree Gully, Whisky Gully). Alluvial output was unimportant after about 1880 [#295, #314, correction].
- Nuggets: about 103 oz from Cobbler's Gully; 56 oz in two pieces from Fern Tree Gully in December 1880; 34 oz 13 dwt at 11 ft [#296, #314, correction]. Junner argued the nuggets were shed from the upper parts of reefs such as the Swedish [#297].
- By the end of the 1860s the Panton Hill goldfield became less profitable and miners turned to farming [#350]. Gold declined by the early 1880s [#99].
- Deep lead mining under basalt at South Morang (1895–1904) was the field's only deep lead period: James Freehold washed five loads for 5 oz 4 dwt in 1895; only Wilton's Freehold gave payable gold before closing in 1904 [#181].
- Nairne & party worked Ghosts Gully (Steeles Creek) in 1908, listed by Bannear as the field's last recorded alluvial mining [#182, correction].

**Quartz reefs.**

- First reefs 1855; first crusher at Back Creek February 1856 [#183]. In 1860 at You You (Yow Yow) Hill, Holmes, Randle and Ricards had a 500-foot tunnel with tramway and a mill of eight stampers with Wilkinson's calcining plant [#184].
- *Swedish Reef, One Tree Hill (1859).* In 1859 one party extracted 15 lb of gold from 60 lb of quartz, and a year later another got over 5 lb from one bucketful [#185]. Junner's table (picked specimens, some figures recorded in pounds weight) gives 144 oz from 90 lb at 21 ft (June 1859), 240 oz from 60 lb (Aug 1859), 80 oz from one bucket at 64 ft (Jan 1860), and in 1865 16.5 tons for 349 oz [#305, correction]. March 1860: about 780 oz from 65 tons [#286]. The January 1861 Mining Surveyor said the owners of No. 2 South were about to abandon it "on the eve of their finding quartz", with patches worth about £8,000 [#306, correction]. Recorded production is only 620 oz against an 1861 estimate of about 2,000 oz [#304, correction]. Production fell off after 1870 [#285].
- *Buck Reef.* 2,126 oz from 3,313 tons between 1866 and 1873 [#283, #307].
- *Mystery Reef.* A fourth line found at depth; 152 tons returned 440 oz (about 2.9 oz/ton) between December 1918 and July 1922 [#200].
- *Lancashire Lass and Homeward Bound lines (Whitelaw 1919).* Lancashire Lass yielded up to 20 oz/ton at shallow depth [#199, correction]. A 40 oz nugget at about 100 ft on the Homeward Bound line was reported verbally to Junner by Mr Hirt [#315].
- *Smile of Fortune Reef.* About a quarter mile NW of the Swedish workings, tested in 1932 by a 93-ft shaft with a 25-hp crude oil engine [#201].
- *Moonlight, Victoria reefs.* Named but not quantified; Victoria prospected late 1850s–early 1860s with "little gold" [#308, correction].
- One Tree Hill minor parties: 1903 small parties; 1904 Hidden Mystery; 1908 Madame Dobson Co. built a crusher but milled only remnants; 1913 Gerlach & party extended a tunnel to reach the Swedes lode [#203]. Hirt and Gerlach drove a tunnel from the head of Cobbler's Gully to find the Swedish reef and appeared not to have found it by Junner's visit [#316, correction; #298]. March 1942: about 115 oz from 21 tons (about 5.5 oz/ton) from the One Tree Hill mine [#202].
- *Oram's Reef, Kingstown/Panton Hill.* Discovered 1858 (Bannear) [#187] or 1859 (Junner) [#302]. By 1864 it was the only vigorous reef on the field, with 6 revolving stampers driven by a 10-hp engine [#187]. Revived 1877–1885 under the Oram's Reef Company, then Bacchante and Phoenix companies, which closed when the rich shoot petered out [#189]. Panton Hills reefs yielded about 9,000 oz to 1890, most of it from Oram's [#302, correction]. Richest ore 100–200 ft [#289, but see correction to #302]; the 300 ft level alone gave about 2,000 oz [#289]. Napoleon, Boomer's and Doctor's reefs gave only "a small quota" [#303].
- *Yow Yow.* Main reefs Allen's (over 2,700 oz), Eureka (over 1,500 oz), Comet, Murray's, No. 5 and Hirt's; total about 8,000 oz [#291, #309, correction]. Allen's reef 2–3 inches wide, locally 2–3 ft, worked to about 200 ft [#292]. The Caledonia shaft at Yow Yow was sunk to 200–300 ft; its thin veins in hard diorite were unprofitable [#294, #310, correction]. By 1864 quartz reefing had virtually stopped on You You and One Tree hills [#187].
- Nickinson's crusher operated at Panton Hill in 1865–66 [#272].

**Twentieth century revivals.**

- Queenstown Government Battery: opened soon after World War I, 5-head, used intermittently. The weatherboard battery house burnt in the 1962 bushfire. Remains (mortar blocks, concrete weir, cyanide relics) are at the start of the Smiths Gully walking track opposite Queenstown cemetery [#195, #108]. Statewide, government batteries cost £52,717 against £7,905 income for 24 plants [#196, correction]. The Golden Stair(s) mine, the most prominent field mine of the 1920s, carted small quantities of rich quartz to the Queenstown battery [#192, correction on locality].
- Black Cameron Mine, Smiths Gully: September 1941 crushing of 21 tons yielded 127.75 oz [#197]; later chronology (1947 shaft to 50 ft; 1948 W. Clayton retimbered to 80 ft then 105 ft, compressor and winder; 1949 over 2 oz/ton; reopened 1951–52; buildings burnt 1962) is unverified beyond the 1941 figure [#197, correction]. As of 1999 it was an active operation on freehold land with the last in situ operating quartz battery in the district [#198, correction]. Golden Crown's success prompted the Black Cameron and the Big Ben mine at Kinglake; Big Ben installed a new crushing plant in 1948 [#194].
- Smiths Gully alluvial workings: three patches of bank sluicing with water races and a hut site, in poor condition, at the location of the field's first rush [#225].

**Heritage at Panton Hill.**

- Alluvial Gold Mining Remains, End Blue House Road (125 Blue House Road), HO12, Place ID 55303 [#364]. On the southern bank of a watercourse running into Long Gully Creek: mullock heaps, excavations and eroded gullies [#365]. The 2009 Statement of Significance calls them the best alluvial gold mining remains in the Shire of Nillumbik [#228]. Protection extends to a 20 m radius [#229]. Assessed in the Shire of Eltham Heritage Study (David Bick, 1992); nearby Inventory sites are Long Gully Settlement and Mining Ruins, Yanggai Gold Mining Area and Bulwidj Gold Mine Shaft [#230, #120].
- The Panton Hill Bushland Reserves brochure says mullock heaps are scattered through one reserve (probably the Yellow Box reserve) and a collapsed mine shaft lies about 20 m off the main trail, in a stand of old-growth Ironbark [#276, #277, corrections].

**Census and population.**

- Queenstown 207 (1871), 1,162 with diggings (1881), 103 (1891), 809 (1901) [#101]. Smiths Gully 39 (1921) to 146 (1961) [#109].

**Round 3 detail: geology of Smiths Gully and St Andrews.**

- Nillumbik Landcare: the gully lies in the Silurian Anderson Creek formation, and the Devonian quartz-diorite plug at The Knobs, St Andrews, is given as the origin of the reef and alluvial gold of the Caledonia diggings [#629]. This agrees with Panton's 1865 note that the Yow Yow veins were in syenite like the Woods Point reefs and that only the veins in diorite were continuous [#543, #544], and with Junner's diorite at Yow Yow [#294]. Gold was found in Smiths Gully in 1854; thousands of miners took up claims and diverted Smiths Gully Creek many times [#626]. The Smiths Gully to St Andrews Nature Trail (3 km return) follows the Old Caledonia Gully diggings from Peter Franke Reserve, the site of Market Square and the State Battery [#627, #628].

**Round 3 detail: Panton's 1865 report and later reef news.**

- The St Andrew's west subdivision covered Anderson's Creek and Caledonia [#536]. Caledonia was the richer field, with recent nuggets of 20 oz and 80 oz from old workings [#540] and a One Tree Hill crushing of over 40 oz/ton [#541]. Several companies had collapsed through mismanagement, but miners worked the same reefs at a profit [#542]. Only seven crushing mills existed, three of them idle [#550].
- Panton's reef table: Oram's (opened 1859 or 1860, highest 23 oz, average 3 oz/ton) and the Boomer Gully reef (1860, highest 33 oz, average 5 oz/ton) [#551]; the Swedish reef (opened 1858, average 4 oz/ton) and the Comet at Yow Yow (1865, 4 oz/ton) [#552, low confidence]. Panton predicted rich veins in the slate spur north of the Yow Yow syenite hill [#545].
- By February 1867 the field was quiet, crushings were poor and many called it worked out; a correspondent still thought the ground between Queenstown and Steel's Creek promising [#505, #506]. The Old Caledonia Comet lode at Queenstown gave 23 oz from a 5-ton trial in 1907 (OCR garbled) [#535]. Plunkett's Long Gully reef at Panton Hill had returned about £2,000 by 1903 [#529].

**Round 3 detail: people, deaths and places.**

- Deaths: Thomas Smith, 26, was killed by a rock fall in the No. 1 North stope of Oram's Reef on 23 July 1877 [#694]; a miner named Baine was smothered in March 1884 [#569]; James Nickinson survived an 1874 cave-in that killed his mate Howard, and said many miners were in deep poverty in 1884 [#570]. Queenstown Cemetery's first recorded burial was in 1861; many early burials, including Chinese, are unrecorded [#631, #507]. Chinese residents sold fireworks at the 1863 Queen's birthday celebrations [#695].
- Names: Queenstown was also called St Andrews by 1865; Panton Hill was Kingstown [#632]. St Andrews emerged in 1854 as the largest township on the field, with over 600 people, was surveyed in 1858 and proclaimed in 1861 [#633]. The Caledonia Hotel became the St Andrews Hotel in 1868 [#692]. Samuel Smith supplied the diggers from 1854 [#693]. Decline was evident by the 1890s [#634]. The Yarra bridge on the Caledonia road was washed away in the 1863 flood [#635].
- Heritage: the Yanggai Gold Mining Area (H7922-0329, Blue House Road) is rated of moderate to high local and state significance; the most intensive workings are on the south side of the gully within 50 m of the creek [#452–#454]. The Long Gully Settlement and Mining Ruins would be of state significance if Chinese mining could be shown [#481]. The Queenstown Government Battery (75 Smiths Gully Road) was a 5-head plant opened soon after the First World War and burnt in 1962; timber mortar blocks, a concrete weir and a cyanide works remain [#457–#459]. State batteries began in 1897 and lost money: £52,717 spent against £7,905 income by 1910 [#455, #456].
- Stan Bone ran the last two independent mines in Smiths Gully, the One Tree Hill and the Black Cameron; a film about him was screened in 2025 [#691]. A 2020 video shows a disused mine on private land near St Andrews with a hand-cart tramway, points and a crane, possibly used into the early 1980s [#737, #738].

- Round 4, One Tree Hill and Yow Yow rush, 1895–1897: in the survey of 1895 the Lancashire Lass reef at One Tree Hill had 5 inches of stone, and the only battery in the district was an eight-head battery at the head of One Tree Creek [#772, #773]. Hopkins and party took 19 lb (304 oz) of gold to the Mint from three tons of quartz in late September 1895 [#930]. A telegram reported 58 oz in a day [#931], and Joyce, Hopkinson and party found 4.5 lb of gold from 7 lb of stone in late October, when the new Queenstown battery opened on 30 October 1895 [#932]. A pocket at about 40 ft yielded 160 oz in December 1895 [#934]. In January 1896 Government Geologist Murray visited the Homeward Bound claim; specimens predicted to yield over 100 oz gave 107 oz [#936, #1143]. By February 1896 Joyce and party had obtained over 1000 oz in a few months [#937]. A 20 oz nugget was found on 8 August 1896 [#941, #1025]. By January 1897 Joyce, Hemmis and Hopkins had won 3500 pounds worth of gold in under half a year [#943, #1150]. In June 1897 Hugo King and party crushed 10 tons from the Yow Yow claim (the old Reliance) for 274 oz [#944]. Rich pockets were met every few weeks on the Homeward Bound reef [#945]. In July 1897 the Birthday Co-operative at Yow Yow Hill had passed into soft decomposed granite [#946]. Antimony was so associated with the gold that 15 to 20 per cent of the gold was said to be lost through defective appliances [#778].
- Round 4, earlier and later One Tree Hill records: the first quartz reef crushed on the Caledonia field in 1855 yielded about 3 oz/ton [#869]. The Victoria reef on One Tree Hill was partly abandoned in mid-1859 [#871]. The September 1859 report counted 588 persons at Caledonia, of whom 180 were Chinese [#872]. In 1884 a resident said the Caledonia alluvial diggings had passed their best days [#911]. In May 1906 the Caledonia Star mine had crushed 203 tons for 674 oz, and the Old Caledonia Co. planned to work five reefs [#800, #801]. The Bourchier Syndicate struck a reef with visible gold in a tunnel at One Tree Hill in October 1919 [#959]. The Queenstown State battery opened in late 1919 [#808]. Crushings of 1921–1922 included 48 oz 18 dwt from 20 tons, 77.5 oz from 26 tons and 42 oz 5 dwt from 15 tons (52 oz 5 dwt in another version, so the yield is uncertain) [#960, #962, #965, #966, #961]. A 1922 rumour of a 500 pound advance was denied [#814]. In July 1932 a party crushed 3 tons at the One Tree Hill battery for 6 dwt/ton [#834]. The Black Cameron extension at Smiths Gully produced almost 15,000 pounds of gold in 18 months to January 1951 [#861].

### 3.7 Yarrambat, Plenty River and Hurstbridge

- Hundreds of Chinese miners on the Plenty River in the late 1800s [#50, #112, correction]. Hugh Glass's Gully on the Plenty at South Morang was an alluvial location by 1865 [#178]. Smugglers Gully (Plenty Gorge) has about 1 km of shallow alluvial workings on the south side with at least five hut sites. Janefield 2 has mining paddocks and adits in quartz gravel ridges; Janefield 3 is an old bluestone track probably to the diggings [#210].
- South Morang deep leads under basalt, 1895–1904 [#181].
- Darnley's Tunnel (H7922-0158) survives as a mullock heap and filled-in tunnel with an air shaft up the hill. The Golden Stairs Company mine and a relocated head frame from Claytons Mine at Yarrambat are listed as locally significant [#209].
- Kenny reported on the Smithfield mine, Yarrambat, in 1932 [#227].
- The 1939–47 productive period began with a new reef outcropping in an orchard at Yarrambat. Prospectors formed the Golden King company with an 8-head battery and oil-engine generator; the Golden Crown was most successful and operated until 1950 [#193]. The council lists Golden Crown (1937), Golden King (1939), Golden Stairs and Golden Gate [#51] and says the Golden King closed in 1984 [#113]. Bannear's 1950 and the council's 1984 refer to different mines and are not in conflict.
- Hurstbridge: Wilton Vale (H7922-0149) is an 1860s homestead with gold mining adits and a dump; Red Crab Mine (H7922-0159) is a tunnel about 70 m long [#208]. Kenny reported on the Three Chums mine, Hurstbridge, in 1938 [#227]. The Templestowe anticline is exposed in the railway cutting south of Hurstbridge station [#313].
- No production figures survive for Yarrambat or Hurstbridge.

- Round 3: in 1903 Atkinson and two other parties got excellent returns from alluvium at South Morang, and almost the whole Plenty River was taken up [#530]. Atkinson's reef at Morang was the northern end of the fourth quartz belt [#522]. Wattle Glen's gullies and hillsides were explored in the mid-1850s, with some small-scale mining [#640].

### 3.8 Christmas Hills

- Gold was discovered at One Tree Hill in 1859, fuelling a brief rush [#353]. Quartz reef operations ended in 1864 [#354], matching Bannear's statement that reefing had virtually stopped on One Tree Hill by 1864 [#187]. Alluvial mining continued at Christmas Hills until 1908 [#355, correction], matching the last alluvial entry in Bannear [#182].
- The One Tree Hill of Christmas Hills (Wikipedia, -37.654, 145.316) and the One Tree Hill of the Caledonia field (Bannear, -37.597, 145.266) are the same hill described from two localities; the coordinates in the claim set differ by about 7 km and one is wrong. The hill lies in the Warrandyte-Kinglake Nature Conservation Reserve, where deer-control closures apply in 2026–27 [#336]. The full reef and alluvial history is in 3.6.
- The track through Christmas Hills was one of the busiest in the colony, with 13 hotels between Eltham and Yarra Glen [#115].

### 3.9 Kinglake: the Mountain Rush of 1861

- In February 1861 David Moore led a party into dense scrub on Mountain Creek, nine miles north-east of Queenstown, just east of present Kinglake. The rush spread over the gullies above Mt Slide on five creeks (Number 1 to 5 Creeks, feeding the Yea River) and was called Moore's Rush or the Mountain Rush [#649, #651]. An Argus letter of 12 July 1861 placed the diggings at about 2,500 ft, on four creeks about two miles apart, every one occupied [#553, #554]. Of about 200 claim-holders, perhaps twenty could save money and fifty more earn wages [#555]. The writer found no quartz in place, only angular quartz in the wash and fine colour almost everywhere, and expected no reef [#556].
- A Mountain Rush post office opened on 7 May 1862 and closed in January 1863 as miners left for Woods Point [#651, #650]. Mining revived in 1864, alluvial work continued to 1879, quartz mining followed at Number 2 Creek, and there was more work in the 1880s and in 1948 [#650]. The Big Ben mine installed a new plant in 1948 [#194]. Extensive diggings remain around Kinglake and in Kinglake National Park [#650]. The Mount Slide Road Mine keeps shafts, trenches and tunnels [#483]. At Mt Robertson, Flowerdale, an intact battery frame, a shaft, an incline tramway and an adit survive [#484]. The Kinglake Heritage Centre has a Mountain Rush display [#652].
- Castella: two small tunnels and an air shaft beside the Yea River were found in 2020. The story is that a timber-mill worker reworked this 1850s–60s mine in the late 1940s for a small amount of gold [#653]. Prospecting is banned in the Yea River and its tributaries [#659].
- Access: Kinglake National Park has no designated prospecting area [#333]. Kinglake is at the northern edge of the study area and is not ranked in section 1.1.

### 3.10 Steels Creek rushes, 1867–1895

- Prospectors from the Caledonia field moved to Steels Creek in the later 1850s. Small rushes went to the head of the creek in 1867 and to the Full and Plenty Creek, just east of Mt Slide Road, in 1868. The area was worked again in the 1893 depression, and Mitchell and Porter opened a new lead lower down the creek in 1895 [#645]. Nine families lived there by 1868 [#639, #638].
- In 1893 "The Vagabond" (Julian Thomas) found about 100 men camped along a mile and a half of the Full and Plenty Creek, sinking shafts on the banks with windlasses and bailing water [#646]. There were no sensational finds: nuggets of 1 to 4 oz, good payable dirt and wages of up to £5 a week, with bucket, pan and cradle [#647]. Tents had given way to bark-roofed log huts, and the reef that was "the matrix of the gold" lay up the creek [#648].
- Chalmers Ridge, off Old Mt Slide Road, was first mined about 1890 and keeps an adit, many shafts, costeans and a water race to a sluicing dam [#482]. Nairne's 1908 work in Ghosts Gully was the field's last recorded alluvial mining [#182]. Steels Creek is at the eastern edge of the study area.

### 3.11 Toolangi and other finds outside the study area

- Muddy Creek, the early name of the Yea River, had "a hundred cradles working" on 22 December 1851 [#644].
- Toolangi, 1903: J. Thomas and Scherber reported gold 1.5 miles from the post office in March 1903, in a lode of porphyritic granite with quartz leaders running east–west for 20 chains; an 80 ft tunnel, a 28 ft crosscut and five shafts showed gold [#655]. By September they reported three payable lodes, the worst assaying 11 dwt/ton, a 10 to 12 ft reef at 25 ft and a 30-acre lease. The outcome is unknown [#656]. Scherber's mill site became C. J. Dennis's Singing Gardens [#657]. A Chinese miner is buried in a shaft at Mick's Creek [#654].
- The Higginbotham field on New Chum Creek (Murrindindi) dates from 1866 [#660]. These places are outside the study area and are recorded for context only.

### 3.12 Templestowe and the lower Yarra (Bulleen, Heidelberg, Studley Park)

- **Earliest reports.** Prospecting at Templestowe followed the 1851 Anderson's Creek discovery, including some work on Unwin's Special Survey, by then the Carlton estate [#1060]. Brough Smyth listed a June 1851 report of gold at "the Deep Creek, near the Yarra", possibly Mullum Mullum (Deep) Creek [#1062]. In 1856 diggers going from Eltham to Anderson's Creek went about fifteen miles round by way of Templestowe because there was no Yarra bridge [#1071]. In 1854 John Robert Wilson built the Doncaster Arms Inn on the track to the Warrandyte diggings [#1065].
- **Carlton Estate and Vallack's Reef, 1858.** In June 1858 rich quartz from Vallack's Reef, on the Carlton estate about a quarter of a mile from Templestowe, was shown in Melbourne [#1094]. The reef first seemed to run east-west and then turned north-south, and some quartz was sent to Anderson's Creek to be crushed [#1095]. In September 1858 the Colonial Mining Journal said Cornish engineers Manuel and Teague had opened the first quartz reef of the Boroondara-Bulleen field there, about 8.25 miles from Melbourne [#1081]. Four miners worked it on a six-week lease and carted quartz 11 miles to Anderson's Creek; each was said to have received over 200 pounds in about eight weeks [#1082]. The reef was 4 to 14 inches thick and dipped at about 60 degrees, with gold, antimony, iron and some copper [#1083]. The journal reported about 22 oz/ton and an estimated 30 tons worth about 2,508 pounds in ten weeks [#1084]. The landowner's agent refused an offer of 1000 pounds an acre for the ground over the reef [#1085].
- **Woodfield reef (Bulleen), 1858.** A week later the Woodfield Boroondara reef was opened on Henry Roberts' land near where Koonung Koonung Creek meets the Yarra [#1086]. It outcropped almost north-south, was five feet thick and had been followed to 30 feet; gold lay in small antimony- and iron-bearing quartz veins on the east wall [#1087]. Roberts leased 44 acres on 29 June 1858 for a 2.5 per cent royalty [#1097]. A 1895 letter to the Age, drawing on mining records, gave the Woodfield reef as 5 feet wide and 4 to 5 oz/ton, worked to about 30 feet [#1096]. At least 40 men were mining gold in Boroondara and Bulleen by late August 1858 [#1098]. Mr Wedell of the Criterion Hotel opened a third mine about two miles east of Templestowe [#1088]. In December 1858 a writer asked for the Anderson's Creek warden's area to be extended, because quartz reefs were worked in the immediate vicinity of Templestowe [#1099]. The Colonial Mining Journal predicted that the whole 12-square-mile schist and quartz belt around Heidelberg, Templestowe and the Yarra would in time prove more or less auriferous [#1090].
- **The 30 oz reef.** The Argus of October 1862 noted that as much as 30 oz of gold to the ton of quartz had long ago come from a worked-out reef near Templestowe, with antimony associated [#1104]. A 1932 article described a reef found at Templestowe in 1861, about a mile west of the township: 3 ft of quartz met at 150 ft that also carried antimony; the lead was lost and never refound [#1102]. A 1941 Age article gave a 2 ft reef about a mile west, yielding up to 30 oz/ton at 160 feet, dated 1864, and noted that other sources say the late 1850s [#1105]. Selwyn's survey map showed a reef 1.75 miles south of the township, 30 oz/ton at 150 feet [#1136]. The Mines Department note of 1933 said the vein ran out north and south, leaving only a close clay joint with neither gold nor antimony [#1156]. A 1933 Age writer placed the reef on land bounded by Templestowe Road, Manningham Road and High Street [#1157]. The depth (100, 150 or 160 ft) and the discovery date (late 1850s, 1861 or 1864) are not consistent between sources [#1102, #1105, #1136].
- **Feathertop Avenue.** A 1982 local column said Feathertop Avenue, near Thompsons Road, was the site of a gold and antimony mine in 1856, where a gold reef was found but soon petered out; no output records were known [#1069]. From 1858 shafts were sunk north of Feathertop Avenue and rich ore was mined, but the gold was tied up with antimony that could not then be separated [#1093]. A small show on the Carlton Estate was worked again between 1880 and 1900 to a depth of 400 feet with a horse whim [#1112]. The antimony mine once had 30 men on the payroll and closed about 1910 [#1152]. The 1869 Brough Smyth record lists antimony with free gold at Templestowe [#1108]. In November 1869 prospectors on Mullum Mullum Creek found antimony ore near the township and six licence applications followed [#1110].
- **Antimony Hill, 1889–1893 (and later).** The Antimony Hill Gold & Antimony Mining Co. began large-scale operations in 1890 with a plant on Ruffeys Creek, and the mine closed in 1915 [#1119]. A dam on Ruffeys Creek at Parker Street supplied the battery and Frue vanners in about 1889–1893 [#1118]. The lode dipped north through a zig-zag No. 1 shaft to 282 feet [#1120]. In August 1892 shareholders heard that about 230 tons of ore had given 14 tons of concentrates and 33 oz of free gold [#1122]. On 13 August 1892 water from old workings burst into a new 231-foot shaft and drowned miners James Young and Louis Grant [#1124]. A 1929 recollection placed the same drowning at Winder's mine in the 1880s [#1111]. The two dates conflict, and the 1892 date rests on contemporary company reports [#1123, #1124]. Weekly reports of 1893 recorded a lode showing gold and 80 bags of antimony ore [#1125, #1127].
- **Phoenix Gold and Antimony, 1896–1897.** The Phoenix Gold and Antimony Company held two leases of 42 acres 2 roods at Bulleen/Templestowe, and in June 1896 was in dispute with the landowners over surface compensation [#1144, #1146, #1147]. In April 1897 the Phoenix Gold Mining Company was reported to have struck 15 to 20 oz/ton by tapping the "Black Reef" [#1151].
- **Heidelberg and Studley Park.** In September 1893 Government Geologist R. Murray said gold occurred in small quantities from Studley Park and Kew out to Heidelberg, Templestowe and Anderson's Creek, but not in payable quantities near Melbourne [#1128]. A 1895 letter recorded payable alluvial gold in the low ground between Templestowe and Heidelberg [#1091]. In April 1894 a miner named M'Hugh and mates sank on a quartz outcrop on the Yarra bank near the Heidelberg Shire hall and found no gold yet [#1133]. Heidelberg residents digging wells found quartz and "the color" [#1134]. A 1894 goldfields summary saw chances at Heidelberg only for experienced miners [#1132]. In 1854 the route to the Plenty diggings ran through Heidelberg and a ford by the Queensborough Hotel [#1068]. An 1895 dialogue named Heidelberg and Templestowe as good places to look along the lost course of the Yarra [#1135]. Rucker's Hill, Northcote, is covered in section 3.23.
- **Other Boroondara claims.** A reef 2 feet thick yielding 1 oz/ton lay near Koonung Creek on the edge of Balwyn [#1114, #1137, #1158]. P. H. Smith told Brough Smyth in 1869 of a quartz lode on his Boroondara farm from which gold had been taken [#1109]. A 1932 article placed the gold-bearing formation from Templestowe through Studley Park to St Kilda [#1155]. Junner's later work linked the acid dykes at Templestowe with those at Diamond Creek, Warrandyte and Kinglake [#956].

### 3.13 Eltham and Research diggings, 1855–1866

- **1856 diggings.** In January 1856 gold broker Richard Warren had been buying gold at Eltham for many weeks [#1070]. In July 1856 a correspondent counted three or four sets of diggings near Eltham: the Old Caledonia (paying fair wages), New Caledonia and new diggings in Bell's paddock [#1072]. Bell's paddock on Dry Creek, three miles from Eltham, gave half a pennyweight to three buckets of washing stuff from holes 8 to 12 feet deep [#1073]. About twenty diggers were there and the largest nugget was 13 dwt [#1074]. The lack of crushing machinery at Anderson's Creek was the only hindrance, in the writer's view [#1075].
- **1857 rushes.** In October 1857 sailors sank a hole five or six miles beyond Eltham, struck gold a few feet down, and about 300 men worked there within a fortnight [#1076]. Two Eltham residents brought about ten ounces from nine or ten days' work [#1077]. A small rush at Rocky Gully, three miles beyond Eltham towards Anderson's Creek, had an unknown yield [#1078]. Dry Creek had been rushed in a small way the year before [#1079]. A local said the Eltham diggings were neglected because they were too near Melbourne [#1080].
- **Research and Swipers Gully, 1861.** In May 1861 government mining surveyor J. Murphy reported a gully about two miles from Eltham with its head at Kangaroo Ground as highly auriferous: wash about 3 ft thick under 14 ft of cover, patchy gold up to 2 oz to the tub, and an average of 2 to 3 dwt to a tub of four buckets [#998]. Murphy considered the Kangaroo Ground quartz reefs the source of the gold in Swipers Gully and Draper's Gully [#999].
- **Wet Gully, 1866 and 1882.** In April 1866 Brookes and others applied for a 25-acre quartz lease at Wet Gully, Eltham, between Eltham and Kangaroo Ground, half a mile from the Diamond Creek reef [#1106]. A reef 4 ft 6 in thick had been struck at 12 feet and followed 20 feet; it was called the largest reef in the district and the Minnie Gold-mining Company was to work it [#1107]. In the June 1882 quarter a new reef about 10 inches thick was discovered near Wet Gully, and a trial crushing gave 1 oz 9 dwt 12 gr per ton [#1004]. A 2 ft vein near Eltham averaged an ounce to the ton [#1104]. A gold reef at Research was to be tested in June 1883 [#768].
- **Outlook.** In 1883 a mining writer said quartz east of Eltham looked attractive but was costly to raise below the water level, so thin rich veins often did not repay the labour [#1113]. The Eltham road in 1874 showed the "general appearance peculiar to auriferous country" [#1001].

### 3.14 Plenty River, South Morang and Greensborough

- **The 1851 Plenty Ranges rumour.** On 26 May 1851 a parcel of gold from two men in the Plenty Ranges was shown in Melbourne, and about 300 people were said to be prospecting the ranges [#1061]. On 1 June 1851 a man showed a watchmaker thirty pieces of yellowish material tested as pure gold, said to come from the Plenty River, and a rush followed [#1063]. In early June Francis Stephen and Dr Miller brought back about 1.5 oz of gold found on the surface, and the Government sent Superintendent Sturt and a surveyor to examine the find [#747, #748]. The Argus of 5 June saw a very pure piece said to be from the Plenty Ranges [#741], and judged the figure of 300 diggers an exaggeration [#740]. Frencham, Walsh and others found a quartz vein in an upheaved granite stratum and kept the exact spot secret [#744, #745, #746]. An 1842 recollection told of an old man called "Gum" secretly digging gold in the Plenty Ranges, but troopers found only bellows and crucibles [#1059]. A 2023 talk claimed gold finds in the Plenty River Gorge in 1848, which is a spoken local claim earlier than any documented find [#862].
- **1854–1855 Plenty Ranges diggings.** In September 1854 G. W. McKenna reported gold diffused from the Plenty River east to Diamond Creek, in small parties of diggers, as scale gold with small nuggets [#749, #1066]. A government surveyor washed a dishful and got a pennyweight, and coarse gold was dug at Back Creek near Haley's station [#750, #1067]. By August 1855 most gold came from Wild Dog Creek, eight miles from the old diggings [#751].
- **Greensborough Ranges rush, 1867.** In January 1867 a rush was reported within a mile or two of the Diamond Creek reefs, in the Greensborough Ranges about two miles from Hill's Diamond Creek Hotel [#753]. Claims included Gibson and Co (35 ft shaft, 3 oz 13 dwt/ton in a trial crushing), Hunt and Co (reef at 30 ft, 6 tons gave 9 oz) and Collins and Co (50 tons gave only 10 oz) [#754]. Dr Phipps found quartz indications on the east side of Diamond Creek about 1867 and abandoned his workings by 1870 [#863].
- **Greensborough as a service town.** Greensborough grew as a commercial centre because of the Caledonia field [#739]. The Greensborough Hotel was built in 1864 [#752]. In 1870 the road to Diamond Creek branched from the Yan Yean road at Greensborough [#763].
- **Greensborough finds, 1896–1927.** In August 1896 three prospectors near Greensborough traced colour for 50 yards, sank 10 ft and struck wash 7 to 8 ft thick showing about 1 dwt to the load [#777]. In June 1897 a Mines Department officer found no defined gutter at two Greensborough alluvial finds [#781]. A May 1896 Age article credited the first discovery of gold in Victoria to Jack Grimshaw near Greensborough; the claim is unsupported elsewhere [#776, #1145], and the Age also said the first gold in quartz was found on the Plenty near Greensborough [#1141]. In July 1918 F. Clayton and party struck a new shoot on Grey's line of reef at Greensborough and crushed 2 tons for 7 oz [#807]. In September 1927 a resident reported gold in an outcrop but gave no location [#822]. The Age of 1896 said the country from Kinglake to Oakleigh and from Yarra Glen to Greensborough was upper Silurian and "distinctly gold bearing" [#1142]. In 1893 geologists were said to expect more reefs in the Plenty ranges between Heidelberg and Kinglake [#1131].
- **South Morang deep lead under basalt, 1887–1897.** In the quarter to September 1887 Wilson and Joyce found a new lead on private property in the parish of Morang [#1115]. The wash was 6 inches to 2 feet deep and yielded 1 to 12 dwt to the load, and the registrar noted it differed from deep leads elsewhere where basalt sits directly on the wash [#1116]. In September 1893 Murray reported a well-defined lead under basalt in the Plenty valley near Morang; at Wilton Vale the wash was tunnelled at about 2 oz to the load, and the river bed just below gave nuggets up to 5 oz [#771, #1126]. In September 1896 the Minister of Mines inspected a 700 ft tunnel under basalt on the west bank; three men won 5.5 oz in a fortnight [#779]. In January 1897 a terrace deposit with 1200 ft of tunnelling had widened from 3 to 17 feet, and a 15 oz parcel of alluvial gold was reported [#1148]. Murray recommended boring for the deep ground ahead and the miners asked the department for bores [#1149]. In September 1930 a syndicate planned a mine on a South Morang property as "Yarrambat No. 2"; prospectors often found gold particles in the river bed there [#830].
- **Mernda, 1939.** On 12 January 1939 a Mernda schoolboy, Peter Drake, found a gold splinter in his foot after bathing in the Plenty River [#1160]. Prospectors came to Mernda [#1161]. His father said the splinter was lost and suspected an old tie pin or brooch [#1162].
- **Plenty, 1950s.** Residents recalled drives running into the Plenty River banks near Memorial Drive and Oatlands Road [#859, #860]. The Silurian Melbourne Formation, host of the quartz-vein gold, overlies the Yan Yean Formation [#865].

### 3.15 Yarrambat and Tanck's Corner, 1876–1941

- **Early history.** Yarrambat (Tanck's Corner, named about 1878) was visited by wood-cutters and fossickers, and much gold was reported won in the Plenty River and Smugglers Gully [#838]. Smugglers Gully was first worked by Chinese miners and was worked again in the 1890s depression, when the government paid unemployed men to fossick [#770]. Quartz mining was small in scale from the late 19th to the late 20th century [#766, #767].
- **Golden Stairs, Tanck's Corner, 1924–1929.** In December 1924 the Golden Stairs Company put 10 tons through the Smiths Gully Government battery for 14 oz 1 dwt 12 gr [#816]. In March 1925 a reef was traced for about 200 ft on the surface about two miles north-west of the old Diamond Creek gold mine, and a winze followed it down [#817]. The nominal capital was 32,000 pounds and the north level reef was 18 inches wide with values to 30 dwt/ton [#818]. In 15 months to April 1925 the company opened 170 ft of reef and sank the shaft to 200 ft [#820]. A crushing from the winze gave about 1.5 oz/ton [#1154]. Another account says a lode was traced for nearly 3,000 ft [#1153]. In 1928–1929 there were clean-ups and extensions: new winding plant, boilers and cages [#823, #824, #827]. Clayton's Reward was re-opened in January 1929 [#825]. F. Clayton's Golden Diorite mine had a 50 ft shaft and a 66 ft drive to a lode 9 ft wide in soft white pipeclay; Clayton was the original prospector of Clayton's Reward and the Golden Stairs [#826]. The Golden Diorite had foul air in March 1929 [#828].
- **1931–1936.** In April 1931 J. Beer and Son claimed to have found the Smuggler's Gully reef, with two parallel lodes [#832]. A city syndicate started a shaft near Yarrambat school in August 1931 [#833]. In March 1933 Barkly and party were taking out a crushing, and the Smithfield Company's shaft had not struck a reef [#835].
- **The 1939 Clayton's reef rush.** After the Golden Stairs closed, W. Clayton bought a shallow shaft opposite Yarrambat School in early 1939; the vein was 6 inches of decomposed quartz at 5 to 6 dwt/ton [#841]. At 50 ft vertical the dyke widened to about 12 ft, with a crushing averaging 35 dwt/ton [#842]. By February 1939 three shafts proved the reef for about 120 ft north and south and 60 ft deep [#843]. John Mooney discovered another reef [#844]. A Melbourne newspaper reported gold at Yarrambat on 1 March 1939 [#845]. In April 1939 Clayton crushed 15 tons for 5 dwt/ton, and his brother crushed 3 tons for 5 oz [#847]. By 30 May the reef had widened to 3 ft and a geologist called the discovery significant [#849]. In July 1939 Burkett and Adams struck the reef, 4 ft wide, in a crosscut [#850]. The Yellow Boy Gold Development company negotiated an option over Clayton's 40-acre lease, and a Mines Department officer reported favourably [#851, #852]. The winze was 26 ft deep with large quartz leaders in July [#855]. The New Pioneer mine had been flooded 40 years earlier [#856].
- **Golden King and Golden Crown battery, 1941.** The Golden King battery was erected on North Oatlands Road in 1941, moved from Spargo Creek [#857]. A dam of one million gallons was built in July 1941 to supply an eight-head battery, and the mine was said to be "very profitable" and might lead to the "lost reef" [#858]. The Golden King is the only known surviving battery by A. Robinson's Soho foundry at Maryborough [#864]. Clayton was owner of the Golden Crown mine [#848, #854].
- **Not found.** No claim in the set supports a nugget discovery at Yarrambat in 1930.

### 3.16 Arthurs Creek and Hurstbridge

- **1885 rush.** In February 1885 alluvial yields at the Arthur's Creek rush were satisfactory and Gracie's Reef was found in Kinraid's paddock [#769].
- **Norton's reef and 1897–1898 crushings.** A 3-ton crushing from Norton's reef in Kinraid's paddock gave 28 oz in May 1897 [#780]. Norton and party were said to have obtained 2000 pounds worth in 1897, and a Collingwood syndicate took leases next to them [#782]. An expert advised sinking No. 1 shaft a further 20 ft to meet a shoot [#783]. Charles Draper leased his whole property to keep out speculators [#784]. The Reward claim crushed another 3 tons in April 1898 [#785]. The Charnwood Gold Mining Company's 5 tons gave 81 oz 5 dwt (over 16 oz/ton) in December 1898 [#787, #788]. Norton and Cooper's 3 tons were crushed at Warrandyte in August 1898 [#786].
- **Starlight, 1899, and 1901–1904.** In June 1899 Davies and Coventry found a reef specked with gold on Dr Buttner's land at the head of Broad Gully; they named it "The Starlight" because it was found by night [#789, #790]. In December 1901 Gardner, Coventry and party crushed 7 tons for over 2 oz/ton [#791]. In May 1903 Gardiner and party crushed 12 tons for 366 oz (30.5 oz/ton) [#792]. By September 1903 the Starlight lease had its own battery [#793]. Other reefs were opened near Diamond Creek and at Tanck's Corner [#794, #795, #796].
- **Arthur's Creek Gold-mining Company, 1906.** The mine (formerly Gardiner's) was found a few years before 1906 by a rabbiter, and the owners took about 20,000 pounds of gold from five shafts [#797]. Manager Williams found the reef ran about 30 degrees east of north, and he picked up a lost shoot south of a break [#798]. In June 1906 a payable sheet was cut at 200 ft; 610 tons were crushed for 400 oz worth 1923 pounds [#802]. The company held a credit balance of 800 pounds and leased 14 more acres [#803]. A monthly return of 1907 recorded 120 tons for 75 oz [#804]. At Hurstbridge in 1906, half a mile north of the town, Hodges and Spry worked ground crossed by three parallel reefs, and a fifth was over 2 ft wide [#799].
- **State battery era, 1920–1938.** Sutherland and Son got 57 oz 8 dwt from 7 tons in April 1920 [#809]. Gardiner and Whelan got 75 oz 8 dwt from 6 tons in November 1920, and 315 oz from 42 tons in 12 months [#810]. Radley and Son crushed 4.5 tons from a Hurstbridge mine for 60 oz 14 dwt in December 1924 [#815]. In April 1925 Radley, Plunkett and Stafford got 70 oz from 5 tons at Arthur's Creek [#819]. The Try Again mine between Hurstbridge and Panton Hill averaged 73 oz 5 dwt/ton in a recent crushing, in 1926 [#821, #1044]. In December 1935 the government prospecting scheme supported 75 men at Queenstown-Hurstbridge [#837]. In May 1938 samples were stolen from a dump at the old Arthur's Creek extended lease [#839].
- **Hurst's lost reef.** A 1938 Argus feature says Henry Hurst crushed a projecting rock with a bullock wagon wheel and found gold-studded quartz, and kept the site secret [#840]. L. T. Luxton's 1939 article puts the find in the 1860s on the Hurst run at Diamond Creek Station (now Hurstbridge) [#846]. A 1941 report suggested the Yarrambat mine might lead to the "lost reef" [#858]. The reef has not been refound in the claims [#840, #846].
- **Wattle Glen, 1931–1934.** A man at Wattle Glen found about 12 pounds worth of gold in 1931, and two miners got 4 oz in a fortnight from old diggings in Second Watery Gully in 1934 [#831, #836].

### 3.17 Kinglake

- **Early dates.** Parks Victoria dates the first gold discovery to 1861 at Number One Creek [#876]. The Victorian National Parks Association says gold was found in the late 1850s [#866]. Moore's Rush of 1861 was made by prospectors from the Caledonian field [#877, #878]. About 200 miners were camped along four creeks [#880]. The post office opened on 7 May 1862 [#885]. The rush was abandoned after Woods Point in late 1862 [#884]. Jack Grimshaw stayed on; in 1896 he had obtained nearly 4000 oz in almost 30 years [#879, #884, #940]. A Queenstown brewer was murdered on 23 May 1862 [#886]. Jimmy Galletly was still living on No. 1 Creek in 1926 [#967].
- **1880s companies.** Deep-shaft mining was set up by city-backed companies at Kinglake, Kinglake Central and Pheasant Creek [#890]. The Kinglake Company bought pumping, winding and crushing plant in 1882 [#893, #894, #895, #896]. In January 1883 its engine shaft was 94 ft and it was 770 pounds in debt [#897, #899]. Its first crushing found the dyke too soft [#898]. A trial crushing in February 1883 gave about 1 dwt to the load [#900]. In September 1883 Hunt Bros of Steele's Creek found a new line of reef near the head of First Creek [#904].
- **Band of Hope and Robert Wallace.** In November 1883 the new Band of Hope Company struck an outcrop about a foot thick "literally studded with gold" [#905]. A crushing of 60 tons gave 46 oz in June 1884 [#910]. The company planned a six-head battery driven by a 25 ft water wheel [#909]. By July 1886 the Robert Wallace Gold Mining Company was working the reef formerly called Band of Hope and driving a tunnel [#916, #917]. Water seepage with no adequate pumping ended the 1880s deep mining, and mining had virtually disappeared by the early 1900s [#889].
- **Later records.** A reef was found in October 1895 [#933]. In July 1903 a company was formed for alluvial ground on No. 1 Creek [#950]. In 1931 two relief-funded parties worked alluvial ground [#971]. Parks Victoria records the Mt Slide Road gold mines as a heritage interpretation site [#977, #978]. Junner noted that dykes at Kinglake are often associated with auriferous quartz veins containing stibnite [#957]. A forum post mentions minor gold shows at Kinglake without dates [#989].

### 3.18 Steels Creek, Dixons Creek and Yarra Glen

- **1892–1898 rush.** In October 1892 the Broomhead brothers struck gold at the head of Steel's Creek, six miles from Yarra Glen, and washed 5 dwt of coarse gold from a 40 ft shaft [#918]. A quartz reef showing gold freely was found in October 1893 [#919]. About 150 men worked the rush in November 1893 [#924]. A reef 4 ft wide gave 1/2 oz/ton from 5 tons [#925]. Old reefs near Pinchgut Creek included the Napoleon (up to 2 oz/ton) and the Shamrock (1/2 oz/ton) [#921]. Hunt Bros and Broomhead had stayed since gold was first found about 25 years earlier [#922]. In January 1894 alluvial diggers earned wages and up to 5 pounds a week with nuggets of 1 to 4 oz [#1016, #1017]. The Hunt men found a quartz vein over 2 ft thick at 50 ft in the Lovie Reef [#1018]. An antimony hill between Yarra Glen and Steels Creek assayed 1 oz 17 dwt of gold per ton [#923].
- **1894–1898.** The Government Geologist said in March 1894 that good gold was being obtained in shallow ground [#926]. The Heidelberg registrar reported wages at 40 ft in May 1894 [#928]. A 12 oz nugget was found in April 1895 [#1024]. A land dispute in October 1895 was ruled in the miners' favour [#929]. In August 1896 a 14 lb gold-sprinkled stone was found at Pinchgut [#938] and the Foster Company won over 7 oz [#1026]. Yarra Glen storekeepers and publicans had bought 20,000 pounds worth of gold in three years [#939]. In July 1898 the Steel's Creek G.M. Co. was extending its shaft [#948].
- **Trap Creek, 1907.** In November 1907 Campbell and Russell found a reef on Trap Creek about six miles from Yarra Glen, traced for 900 yards by three shafts [#952]. It widened from 8 to 18 inches and was tested at 4 to 5 oz/ton [#953]. An older reef a mile further up crushed 25 (presumably oz) to the ton, but stone had to be carted to Yea [#954].
- **1930s and later.** In October 1930 the Hurstbridge Advertiser said Steel's Creek promised a revival [#968]. Lond and Owen had encouraging prospects in November 1930 [#970]. Traces of gold were reported at Dixon's Creek in October 1930 [#969]. An antimony mine flourished briefly during the Second World War [#972]. The quartz-feldspar porphyry was dated at 374 million years [#974]. Drill holes of 1989 returned 0.28 and 0.02 ppm Au [#975, #976]. An exploration licence for about 16 square kilometres was applied for in 2013 [#980]. Heritage sites include the Mt Jerusalem Track Mine and the Antimony Mine off Mount Slide Road [#981, #983]. Bannear lists Steele's Creek and Kinglake among the key mining locations of the Caledonia Goldfield [#984]. Yarra Glen (Yarra Flats) was a staging point on the Woods Point track [#875, #881, #882].

### 3.19 Kangaroo Ground

- Fossickers on their way to Queenstown prospected Kangaroo Ground in the 1850s but did not stay [#992]. The Caledonia Hotel opened in 1857 [#993]. In October 1859 a letter writer questioned the Evelyn Quartz-Crushing and Mining Company [#996]. Murphy (1861) considered the Kangaroo Ground quartz reefs the source of gold in Swipers Gully [#999].
- Murray (c. 1894) reported a basalt area at Kangaroo Ground under which auriferous drifts were said to have been traced but not extensively worked, and he saw a good field for prospecting [#1019]. He described a well-marked auriferous belt from Ringwood through Warrandyte, Kangaroo Ground and Panton Hill to Kinglake [#1020, #920].
- Gold was still found in Watery Gully in the 1890s, though resources were running out [#1022]. A small rush in 1900 pegged a dozen or more claims at Stony Creek below Weller's Hotel [#1029]. In June 1908 gold was found while sinking for water on a reef thought to continue the Anderson's Creek reefs, and a Melbourne syndicate pegged leases [#1031]. In 1913 a dam supplied sluicing water to the Pretty Hill Gold Mining Company, and a 2 ton crushing from Pretty Hill gave about half an ounce [#1041, #1042]. A selector at Pretty Hill was listed as a miner in 1876 [#1002].
- Jutson judged reefs outside the Warrandyte area to be thin and irregular [#1035]. The Kangaroo Ground Chronicle says gold was later found at Kangaroo Ground and Watsons Creek [#1056]. Two farm-based reports mention a small gold mine and panning near Nicholas Lane [#1057, #1058], and a childhood memoir recalls gold panning in the Pigeon Bank creek [#1053].

### 3.20 Ringwood (gold and antimony mine, 1869–1936)

- Antimony was discovered at Ringwood in 1869 while road metal was being extracted [#1000]. The mines were suspended in June 1881 [#1003]. By September 1882 there were three antimony companies, and the Ringwood Company had cut a fine antimony vein and a rich quartz vein at 400 ft [#1005]. Because of the gold in the ore, a small battery was considered in December 1882 [#1006]. By 1883 three mines had shifted from antimony to gold [#1007, #1011]. Hodgson's mine, operating from 1871, attempted to extract gold in 1883 [#1008]. On 28 August 1883 a 10-head battery opened; six shafts ranged from 80 to 373 ft, and 3 cwt of stone assayed at Footscray was reported at 43 oz of gold a ton [#1009]. The claim had been worked for 10 years mainly for antimony [#1010].
- The amount of gold extracted is not known, and mining is believed to have ceased by the 1890s depression [#1015]. An August 1896 account saw a gold and antimony mine beside the railway line [#1027]. In 1908 the old mine was said to have once employed nearly 100 men [#1032]. A 1920 attempt to restart it failed [#1043, #1015]. Seven shafts were still visible in 1936 and an area of abandoned mines was reserved in 1929 [#1048]. In 1939 reported gold finds at Ringwood, Croydon and Balwyn were discouraged by the Mines Department [#1049]. A February 1931 unemployed party sent a sample to assay [#1045].

### 3.21 Wonga Park, Croydon, Mount Evelyn and Kalorama

- **Wonga Park and Brushy Creek, 1931–1932.** In March 1931 a Caulfield unemployed party prospecting at Wonga Park found an indicator that grew richer and arranged a trial crushing [#1046]. In February 1932 Mrs A. J. Marchesini, aged 63, was sinking a shaft on her Wonga Park property hoping to strike alluvial gold [#1047]. The 1896 Age listed alluvial deposits at Brushy Creek [#1027]. Jutson described a silicified sandstone band through Birts Hill and along Brushy Creek [#1037]. A 2006 heritage study lists water wheel foundations and a powder magazine in the Wonga Park section of Warrandyte State Park, not inspected [#1052]. Moore (1965) recorded that the centre of the Warrandyte dome was strongly mineralised [#1050].
- **Croydon.** The parish of Warrandyte ran to Croydon, so old records labelled Warrandyte may refer to the Croydon area [#991]. A February 1887 rumour of a Croydon gold lead proved to refer to Queensland [#1013]. Jutson traced the eastern leg of the Warrandyte Anticline to a little north of Croydon [#1036] and noted kaolin workings in dykes near Croydon [#1034].
- **Mount Evelyn and Kalorama.** Some selectors on the Olinda Creek flats held land on miners' rights in the 1890s [#1014]. A filled-in shaft near Priestley Crescent was recalled [#1051]. A 106 ft tunnel near Olinda Creek Road, Kalorama, was thought to be dug by Chinese miners [#1054]. The Mount Evelyn History Group could not positively identify any mine site there [#1055]. Lilydale and Mooroolbark claims describe only goldfield traffic and a silver find [#994, #995, #997, #1028].

### 3.22 Christmas Hills and Watsons Creek

- Miners fossicked the Christmas Hills in the 1850s [#867]. Buttermans Track is believed to have been opened to serve the Caledonian fields [#868]. A December 1859 report recorded a new gully in the parish of Nillumbik falling into Watson's Creek a mile above the Yarra [#873]. The January 1863 report recorded 515 Europeans and 46 Chinese, 110 quartz miners and 42 distinct reefs, and a rush to Watson's Creek [#887]. Watsons Creek is named after Sandy Watson, who sold meat and butter to miners [#990].
- One Tree Hill (Christmas Hills): the Queenstown Quartz Mining Company sank on the underlay in 1884 [#912], and the Queenstown Gold Mining Company was fitting up machinery in 1885 [#914]. A 2 oz piece of quartz with 10 dwt of gold found on the old main road in September 1897 was said to have come from Western Australia [#947]. The "Mystery" claim gave 8 oz 8 dwt from 9 tons in 1903 [#949]. In June 1922 the One Tree Hill Gold Mine reported a reef widening at 60 ft [#963, #964]. Victorian Places says Christmas Hills as a whole did not have much goldmining, though One Tree Hill was a mining site [#987]. A private mine beside the Happy Valley track is still active, and a 160-year-old shaft lies beside the Ridge track [#985, #986]. A miner worked underground at One Tree Hill until the 2009 fires [#979]. Small mining operations continued in the mid-1900s [#973].

### 3.23 Where there is no gold

The widened search also recorded places where the evidence is negative or absent. These are as useful for prospecting as the positive records.

- **Kew and Studley Park.** A coarse speck of gold was found in gravel at Studley Park in 1861, but a 15 ft shaft on the hill found narrow quartz veins and no gold, and the gold-bearing gravel bank did not continue into the hill [#1100, #1101]. Murray (1893) said gold occurred in small quantities from Studley Park out to Heidelberg but not in payable quantities near Melbourne [#1128].
- **Government House to Heidelberg.** Murray noted a marked absence of quartz in sufficient quantity between Government House and Heidelberg and saw no geological indication of a payable discovery [#1129].
- **Heidelberg.** M'Hugh's 1894 sinking near the Shire hall found no gold yet [#1133]. The 1894 summary said experienced miners would struggle to make a living [#1132].
- **Doncaster.** The claims about Doncaster describe the Doncaster Arms Inn on the track to the Warrandyte diggings, upper Silurian country except an ironstone and quartz conglomerate, and small reefs linked to diorite dykes less pronounced there than at Wood's Point or Walhalla [#1065, #1138, #1159]. The 1939 Mines Department investigation of reported gold finds at Ringwood, Croydon and Balwyn discouraged them [#1049].
- **Northcote.** Murray said Rucker's Hill rocks were of the auriferous order and a small payable reef might be found, though he was not sanguine [#1130]. The 1896 Age reported the alluvium there contained gold [#1139], and a deep lead was believed to pass under Northcote [#1140]. No mine is recorded.
- **Inner northern suburbs and other localities.** The claim set has no gold records for Fitzroy, Brunswick, Coburg or other inner northern suburbs. This is an absence of records, not proof of absence of gold.
- **Not gold.** The Lilydale Cave Hill find of 1897 was silver [#1028], and the Croydon lead of 1887 was in Queensland [#1013]. The Donnybrook crushing at Deep Creek in 1852 gave 4 oz from 4 tons, and the Bolinda Mining Company formed in 1859 [#1064]. Mooroolbark has only goldfield traffic [#997]. The Mernda splinter of 1939 was probably not natural gold [#1162].

## 5. Modern prospecting

### 5.1 Legal framework

- A Miner's Right is required for recreational prospecting anywhere in Victoria and must be carried at all times. The exception is an organised group under a Tourist Fossicking Authority [#55, #328, #235]. It costs $26 from Services Victoria and lets the holder keep any gold found [#338]. Rights and obligations are set by the Mineral Resources (Sustainable Development) Act 1990 [#329, correction]. Almost 73,000 Miner's Rights were active in Victoria and more than 8,000 were bought in 2021 (figures from the page's February 2023 publication) [#340, correction].
- Penalties for prospecting without a Miner's Right are up to $36,984 [#339]. Earth Resources Regulation inspectors and Conservation Regulator officers check licences, permitted areas and environmental impact [#343].
- Where allowed: state forests and designated areas within listed national, state, historic and heritage parks [#231, #341, #347]. Prospecting is banned on prohibited Crown land, which includes parks under the National Parks Act 1975 (outside designated areas), Aboriginal Heritage Act 2006 declared areas, Reference Areas and the Deep Lead Nature Conservation Reserve near Stawell [#232, correction]. It is also banned in certain listed streams, creeks and rivers; check the linked list before panning [#234, correction].
- Consent: other Crown land can be prospected without land-manager consent, though confirmation is advised. Private land needs the owner's or occupier's consent. Mining, prospecting and retention licence areas need the licence holder's consent; exploration licence areas do not. Consent may carry conditions and may be withdrawn [#233]. Licences near a site can be checked on the "Resource Licences near me" tool [#236, correction].
- Heritage: under the Heritage Act 2017 it is an offence to damage a VHR place without a permit, or to disturb any historical archaeological site or remove artefacts. A Miner's Right gives no rights under the Heritage Act [#330, correction]. Under the Aboriginal Heritage Act 2006, cultural heritage found while prospecting must be reported to First Peoples-State Relations; harming it is an offence [#331].
- Earth Resources Regulation publishes a Guide to Recreational Prospecting and a video [#345]. Closures can be checked on MapshareVic [#344, correction].

### 5.2 Permitted sites in the study area

- **Warrandyte State Park: beds of the Andersons, Stoney and Jumping Creeks**, upstream of the Jumping Creek Road bridge [#323, correction]. Round 1 recorded only the Fourth Hill section of Andersons Creek [#55, #57]. A Miner's Right is required and mechanical tools are prohibited [#324, #56]. Excavation only in the creek bed; backfill holes; do not disturb vegetation [#57]. Parks Victoria calls Andersons Creek "a popular location for prospecting" [#58]. Warrandyte State Park is the only park in the core study area on the Resources Victoria list [#231] and the only one near Nillumbik with a Parks Victoria prospecting guide (PDF) [#333]. RACV: finds "are usually small" [#346].
- Other Fourth Hill rules: no dogs, camping, fires, generators, firearms or drones without a permit [#59]. Hazards: water, flash flooding, falling limbs [#60].
- Minimum-impact rules: prospect only in the permitted area, drive only on open public tracks, park on the roadside, take rubbish home, minimise damage to ground-layer vegetation, backfill holes and restore leaf litter [#332, #342]. Use hand tools only (pans, sluices, metal detectors); no explosives; avoid digging in historic sites [#348, #342].
- **Night closures.** Deer control from 13 July 2026 to 30 June 2027: Jumping Creek Reserve, Mt Lofty Reserve, Pound Bend Reserve and The Common [#61]; also One Tree Hill, Rifle Range Reserve (Warrandyte-Kinglake NCR) and the Kinglake Biolink. 4 pm to 6 am on control nights, excluding weekends, public and school holidays; signs at entrances; enquiries 13 1963 [#336, correction]. The Jumping Creek shelter is closed until further notice [#337].
- The 5 October 2026 rule changes affect the Central West and Mirboo North only. Warrandyte is not listed [#335].
- The 2006 Warrandyte State Park Management Plan is the legal basis. It designates 6.5 ha of creek bed (1% of the park) under Section 32D of the National Parks Act, for hand tools, pans and simple cradles, with backfilling required and vegetation left undisturbed. It lists metal detectors among the permitted equipment in the creek beds [#493, #494]. Old shafts and adits at Whipstick Gully, Fourth Hill, Black Flat and the Timber Reserve are closed hazards [#498].
- No other site in the study area is confirmed as open. Mine Hill, Collard Drive, Yarrambat, Smiths Gully, Panton Hill and St Andrews are private, council or park land with no stated permission. Kinglake National Park has no prospecting information on the Parks Victoria page [#333].

### 5.3 What is banned or restricted

- Mechanical tools in Warrandyte State Park [#56, #324].
- Digging outside the creek beds, leaving holes open, disturbing vegetation [#57, #332].
- Entering Geraghty's Mine beyond the gate [#86]. The park's former mines are closed to the public [#268].
- Disturbing the Pound Bend Gold Diversion Tunnel (VHR H1260) [#162, #75, #78, #156] or the surrounding workings (H7922-0174) [#75].
- Disturbing Heritage Inventory and Heritage Overlay sites: Bulwidj Gold Mine Shaft [#120], Nillumbik Mine [#91], Blue House Road remains (HO12, 20 m buffer) [#229, #364], Sailor's Reef and Great Southern (HO71, HO84) [#163], Wilton Vale, Red Crab Mine, Darnley's Tunnel [#208, #209], the Pound Bend and Fourth Hill relics [#325].
- The Yarra River bed itself is not in the permitted list [#323].

### 5.4 Where people report finds

- No modern (post-1984) specific find is in the claim set. Parks Victoria's "popular" description [#58] and eMelbourne's note of weekend prospectors at Pound Bend and Whipstick Gully in the 1970s [#259] show continued activity. RACV says finds are small [#346].
- The Black Cameron mine at Smiths Gully was an active commercial operation as of 1999, planning to crush One Tree Hill ore [#198, correction].
- Historic nuggets: 100 oz at Warrandyte in 1874 [#237]; about 103 oz in Cobbler's Gully and 56 oz in Fern Tree Gully (1880) on the Caledonia field [#296, #314]; a 40 oz nugget reported at 100 ft on the Homeward Bound line [#315]. Parks Victoria contrasts the Golden Triangle of central Victoria, source of many of the world's largest nuggets [#334, correction].
- Old mine waste is believed to form gravel beaches near the Diamond Creek railway line [#90].

### 5.5 Methods indicated by the history

- Alluvial: the 1851 gold was panned from Anderson's Creek gravel banks [#12, #212]. Stoney Creek was worked by 1860 [#177]. River sluicing used cofferdams pumped by waterwheels [#218]. Silt up to 14 m deep covered the Pound Bend gravels [#151]. Caledonia alluvial gold was coarse, with nuggets shed from reef tops [#186, #297]. Alluvial was largely exhausted there by 1880 [#295].
- Reef: thin (under 1 ft), near vertical, striking N10E–N25E at Warrandyte [#134]; N15–30E at One Tree Hill [#282, #285]; N25E at Oram's [#287]. Gold shallow at Warrandyte [#130] but richer at depth at Oram's [#302]. Stibnite in quartz veins cutting altered dykes is a field indicator east of Melbourne [#311, correction]. Flat pinkish quartz "indicators" may localise gold in the Swedish reef [#300].
- Dyke: low grade (about 1 dwt 14 gr per ton) at Warrandyte [#129]; the Diamond Creek dyke was far richer [#191, #204].
- At the permitted sites only creek-bed panning and hand sluicing is possible [#56, #57, #324].

## 6. Rumours, legends, theories and predictions

Each item is labelled by type and provenance.

**Rumour: gold found at Warrandyte from 1841.**
The Historical Society says finds are "said to have been made from as early as 1841 but were not reported" [#141]. Bannear also refers to reports in the Plenty Ranges from 1841 [#213, correction]. Hearsay.

**Rumour / tradition: first gold licences in Victoria issued at Anderson's Creek.**
Wikipedia [#153] and eMelbourne [#256]. Bannear dates the first licences there to September 1851 [#212]. Now supported by three sources, but the colony-wide licence system also began in September 1851 [#153, correction].

**Contested claim: Warrandyte as Victoria's (or Australia's) first gold discovery.**
Parks Victoria [#54], the 1935 memorial [#7], the Historical Society [#138], local media [#260, #261]. The Historical Society's Victorian Collections page says Michel was "the first person to discover gold in Australia" [#278]; this is an overstatement [#278, correction]. Clunes (7 July 1851 per eMelbourne) is the main rival [#254]. Safe wording: "first officially recorded or declared" [#138, correction].

**Legend: Michel's "large nugget".**
The Manningham brochure says Michel "uncovered a large nugget" [#252]. Michel's own account describes 10 small grains [#83]. Prefer Michel.

**Michel's own claim: the discovery slowed emigration to New South Wales.**
From heritage walk signage [#83]. Not established.

**Competing discovery accounts at Smiths Gully and the Caledonia field.**
Boston (late 1854) [#105]; the Smythe brothers [#107]; August 1854 Caledonia fields [#117]; 1854 Stringy-bark Forest [#270]; 1854 streams into Watsons Creek [#275]; early 1855 rush [#174]; 1855 "further upstream" [#263]. All agree on late 1854 to early 1855.

**Competing discovery accounts at Diamond Creek.**
Wilson brothers 1862 [#28]; Peers, Haley and Wilson 1863 [#46, #92]; Diamond Reef 1863 [#262]; "early 1860s" [#164].

**Legend: the name Diamond Creek.**
Crystalline stones [#97]; a bullock named Diamond [#97, correction].

**Local belief: mine waste beaches at Diamond Creek.** [#90].

**Local colour: the Union winder foundations "guarded by red-bellied black snakes".**
WikiNorthia [#168]. Descriptive, not evidential.

**Hearsay production figure: £60,000 worth of gold from one shoot at the Union adit.**
Dunn (1905) via Bannear says an earlier party "is said to have obtained" it [#204, correction]. At £4/oz this is about 15,000 oz. Unverified.

**Hearsay: payable stone of 2–3 oz/ton south of the Union, flooded.**
"Said found" in 1905 [#205]. Never worked.

**Rumour: a 40 oz nugget on the Homeward Bound line.**
Reported verbally to Junner by Mr Hirt [#315, correction].

**Theory (Jutson 1910): reef lengths match fold lengths.** [#137]. The predictive use is the compiler's inference [#137, correction].

**Theory (Jutson 1910): minor folds, not the dyke, limit the field.** [#136].

**Theory (Whitelaw, via Jutson): a Warrandyte dyke reaches Kangaroo Ground.** [#133]. Unconfirmed; dykes low grade [#129].

**Theory (Junner 1914): Caledonia nuggets were shed from reef tops.**
Junner argued the alluvial nuggets came from the upper parts of reefs such as the Swedish [#297]. Supports looking for reef above nugget gullies.

**Theory (Junner 1914): gold in the Swedish reef is localised at "indicator" intersections.** [#300].

**Prediction (Junner 1914): gold does not stop at depth on the Caledonia field.**
No geological evidence that gold stops at depth; secondary enrichment probably not major [#299]. Few workings went below the water table; the Swedish reef was not prospected below 200 ft [#298]. Early mines were abandoned shallow because individuals, not companies, worked them; small companies could work some at a profit [#301]. Oram's Reef, the only reef worked below 300 ft, got richer with depth [#302, correction]. This is the strongest unexploited-potential statement in the claim set.

**Prediction (Kenny, via Bannear): a five-head battery at One Tree Hill if development succeeded.** [#200]. The Mystery Reef gave 2.9 oz/ton in 1918–22.

**Observation with predictive value (Jutson, Bannear): shallow gold reflects where miners stopped.**
Pre-1898 Warrandyte reefs were worked to the water table and abandoned [#130, #219]. The Caledonia then took gold from the sulphide zone [#130, correction].

**Failed predictions: Pound Bend and The Island.**
The Evelyn company expected to profit from five kilometres of riverbed [#22]; no dividend [#17]. The Yarra Yarra company's canal was defunct within two years [#217]. Hydro proposals failed [#18, #19, #24, #25].

**Failed prediction: the Diamond Creek Company at Phipps Reef, 1882.**
Collapsed "without even striking a pick" [#190].

**Failed prediction (1950s): reopening the Diamond Creek mine.**
The 1950s company pumped the flooded shaft, stopped, and left shareholders with worthless certificates [#171]. The 1915 judgement that reopening was almost impossible [#37] held.

**Inference (compiler): alluvial flats as placer setting.**
VNPA describes gum-forested alluvial flats between steep slopes in the Yarra valley [#371]. The link to placer gold is the compiler's inference, not VNPA's statement [#371, correction].

**Inference (compiler): regional crustal structure.**
Willman et al. (2010) tie gold distribution to crustal geometry in the Stawell and Bendigo zones [#320]. The paper does not discuss the Melbourne Zone localities; any local application is an inference.

**Source typo: Pound Bend tunnel "ceased operations in 1972".**
Heritage timeline [#247]. The company was wound up in September 1872 [#17]. Treat as a typo.

### 6.1 Modern news: old mines still in the landscape (2018–2023)

**News: a Warrandyte house sold with its own Gold Rush-era mine (2022).**
A realestate.com.au feature (Alesha Capone, 7 June 2022) describes "Yarra Mist", 80-84 Webb Street, Warrandyte: 1.94 ha bordering Warrandyte State Park, auctioned 18 June 2022 with a $2.1m–$2.3m guide. The selling agent said "There is a gold mine on the property, as you walk out the gate into the state park you can see six gold mines in a space about 50m of each other", and that the mine on the block "has been semi-filled, you can see the opening" [#403]. No mine name, depth or type is given. The Victorian Heritage Database lists other mine sites on Webb Street in the park (for example the Pigtail Mine, HO179), which is indirect support [#403, correction]. The article also calls Whipstick Gully "the site of Victoria's first gold discovery in 1851"; standard sources put the 1851 Michel find at Andersons Creek below Fourth Hill, so treat that wording as loose [#404].

**News: Tills Road shaft rescue (2018).**
On 5 January 2018 a 13-year-old boy climbed into an old mine shaft on Tills Road, Warrandyte, to retrieve his phone, and was rescued unhurt after about an hour (SBS News / AAP) [#407]. Tills Road is the Black Flat area, where the heritage listing records shafts, a puddling machine and a dam wall [#394]. The story shows that unfenced shafts are still open in the park.

**News: Nillumbik Mine, Scrubby Creek, Diamond Creek.**
A Nillumbik Shire Council information sheet places the Nillumbik Mine on the west side of Scrubby Creek along the east side of Collard Drive (75-79 Collard Drive; Victorian Heritage Inventory H7922-0242). The lease triggers contamination assessments for nearby lots [#408]. This is the modern address for the Diamond Creek dyke mine discussed in section 3.3.

**News: Warrandyte Pure Gold exhibition (2022).**
The Warrandyte Historical Society marked 170 years since the 1851 find with an exhibition at its Yarra Street museum, 18 April to 22 May 2022 [#409].

**Legend (outside Nillumbik): the Blackburn mine under Lake Road and Halley Street.**
A Herald Sun feature (Mitchell Toy, 9 November 2023) retells a "local legend" of a disused gold mine beneath Lake Road and Halley Street, Blackburn, near the southern end of Blackburn Lake Sanctuary. It is said to have worked from the 1850s–60s until about 1880, to have failed from water seepage, and to have been filled in by Isaac Champion in 1904 [#405]. The direct answer to "how was it located" is: not by any modern method. The story rests on early-1960s newspaper articles about a school excursion to the site, and on the oral history of Ray Champion, Isaac's son, who said pumping gear was left rotting in the scrub at the back of his property. The article says the mine was "believed to be noted" on old Mines Department maps but cites none. There are no mine plans, no shaft-database record and no heritage listing. The Whitehorse Historical Society record (Victorian Collections ND3749, "Old gold mine site is found") says only that "local history investigators" located and identified the site [#406]. The Herald Sun's own verdict: "Proof is elusive. There are no available records of gold being found at Blackburn." Blackburn is about 12 km south-west of Warrandyte and outside the study area; the item is here as a comparison for how thin the evidence behind suburban mine legends can be.

### 6.2 How to locate old shafts (method note)

The Blackburn case shows what is missing when a legend has no paper trail. For Nillumbik and Warrandyte the paper trail exists, and these are the tools for using it:

- **GeoVic / Earth Resources (Resources Victoria).** The free GeoVic map viewer has "mine shaft" and "historic mine site" layers from the Victorian mines database, plus the geological map sheets. Turn the layers on, zoom to Warrandyte, Diamond Creek or St Andrews, and each recorded shaft or adit is a point with a name and a reference. Not every shaft was recorded, and positions can be tens of metres out.
- **Victorian Heritage Database and Heritage Inventory.** Every heritage-listed mining site in this report (Pound Bend tunnel, Black Flat, the Diamond Creek mine, the Caledonia field sites) has a VHD or VHI record with a description and often a sketch plan [#5, #394, #408].
- **The 1909 and 1893 maps in section 9, overlaid on the modern map.** The 1909 Department of Mines sheet names the reef lines, anticlines and principal quartz workings; the 1893 Geological Survey sheet marks alluvial workings, quartz reefs and the dyke. The page overlays both on the modern map so that a named working can be read against today's streets and the park boundary.
- **PROV mining lease plans.** Public Record Office Victoria holds Mines Department lease plans and company records; a lease number from a VHD entry or from Jutson 1910 can be searched there for the surveyed outline of a claim.
- **On the ground.** Old shafts in this district appear as round or square depressions, often ringed by a low mullock heap, usually on the line of a reef and on the slopes of the hills named in section 3.1 (Fourth Hill, Fifth Hill, Whipstick Gully, Black Flat). Do not enter them; the 2018 Tills Road rescue is the reason [#407].

### 9.1 "Warrandyte Goldfield", Department of Mines, Victoria, [1909?]

- **What it shows.** A single sheet of the Warrandyte field at a scale of chains (80 chains, about 1.6 km, across the scale bar), from Harris Gully and Sailors Gully in the south-west to Wonga Park and Break of Day Gully in the east, with the Yarra River loops at Pound Bend and the Caledonia bends. The whole area is labelled Silurian sandstone and shale. The map draws the fold axes as labelled lines: the Warrandyte (Main North) and Warrandyte (Main South) anticlines, the Caledonia anticline, the Consols anticline, the Fifth Hill anticline and the syncline between them. Dip-and-strike ticks give the bedding dip in degrees. A long straight line marks the dyke running north-west to south-east across the Yarra. Small crosses ("xxxx") mark the principal quartz workings, and circles mark conglomerate beds; "F" marks fossil sites.
- **Named features.** Nth Caledonia, Caledonia, Black Swan, Grant, South Caledonia, Bendigo, Nth Victory, Victory, Specimen Gully, First to Fifth Hill, Whipstick Gully, Ross Gully, Whitelaw Gully, Wild Cat Gully, Fiddler Gully, Thomson, Reward, Consols, Caledonia Consols, Pigtail, Hastings, Scrubby, Eagle, Blocks, Devonshire, Sailors Reef, Great Southern, Contorted Strata, Beauty Gully, Parson Gully, Narrmeian Creek and the State School.
- **Why it matters.** The reef gold at Warrandyte sits on the anticlinal axes and the "principal quartz workings" crosses follow them. Reading the map, the lines of old workings run with the anticlines, not across them, and the richest workings (Victory, Caledonia, Consols) sit where the axes cross the hill crests between Andersons Creek and the Yarra. The legend states that "Recent deposits and alluvial gold workings not shown", so this sheet is for reef lines, not for creek gold.
- **Date note.** The State Library dates the sheet [1909?] from the geology sources it cites (Forbes 1899, Jutson, Howitt 1909). The base says it was compiled from Lands Department surveys "and aerial photographs", so the printing is probably later than 1909; the geology is from the 1899–1910 sources.
- **Source and licence.** State Library Victoria, https://handle.slv.vic.gov.au/10381/117472 (viewer https://viewer.slv.vic.gov.au/?entity=IE15489100). Full-resolution image 5517 x 7000 px. Licence statement: "This work is out of copyright". Credit: State Library Victoria, out of copyright.

### 9.2 Geological Survey of Victoria, County of Evelyn, Quarter Sheet No. 40 N.W. (R. A. Moon, 1893)

- **What it shows.** A hachured topographic and geological sheet at two inches to the mile (about 1:31,680) covering parts of the parishes of Bulleen, Warrandyte, Ringwood, Scoresby and Nunawading: from Doncaster and Box Hill in the west to Croydon and Brushy Creek in the east, and from the Yarra at Warrandyte in the north to Dandenong Creek in the south. Surveyed by R. A. Moon, Field Geologist, under R. A. F. Murray, Government Geologist; lithographed by J. M. Coakley; published under A. W. Howitt, June 1893.
- **Legend.** Green: Alluvial (recent fluviatile and swamp deposits). Yellow: Pliocene (Flemington and Upper Brighton beds, gravel and clay). Buff: Upper Silurian sandstones, slates, shales and mudstones. Red stipple: "thin covering and outliers of Tertiary on older rocks". Yellow stipple: "Alluvial gold workings". T-symbol: strike and dip. Solid line: "Quartz reefs". Red line: "Intrusive dyke". Circled numbers: rock specimens kept at the Department of Mines. Barometer heights in feet.
- **Why it matters.** This is the only sheet in the set that marks alluvial gold workings with their own symbol. The Warrandyte section at the top of the sheet shows the quartz reefs and the dyke (the "Mullum Mullum" and Warrandyte dykes drawn in red) and the Silurian strike. Where the yellow stipple sits on a creek, people were washing gold there in 1893, which is a direct pointer to the gravels that still carry fine gold.
- **Two copies.** The State Library holds two copies of the sheet. The page uses the clean copy (`gsv-1893-moon-sheet2.jpg`); the other copy is hand-annotated and stamped in 1984.
- **Source and licence.** State Library Victoria, https://handle.slv.vic.gov.au/10381/93146 (viewer https://viewer.slv.vic.gov.au/?entity=IE8752291). Full-resolution image 7000 x 5670 px. Licence statement: "This work is out of copyright". Credit: State Library Victoria, out of copyright.

### 9.3 J. T. Jutson, 1910: Plates XCI, XCII and XCIII

- **Plate XCI, "Sketch map of parts of the Parishes of Nillumbik, Sutton, Warrandyte, Ringwood, Bulleen and Nunawading".** The regional structure of the Silurian rocks: fold axes, the dykes and the river system from Diamond Creek down to Warrandyte. It is the only map in the set that extends north into the Nillumbik parish and ties the Warrandyte anticlines to the Diamond Creek and Kangaroo Ground country.
- **Plate XCII, "Enlarged map of, and section across, the Warrandyte goldfield".** The Warrandyte field at a quarter-mile scale with the anticline axes dashed, the dyke line, the quartz workings as dots, conglomerate as circles, and a numbered key (I–XVII) of mines and shafts. A cross-section A–B along the left edge shows the folds under the Yarra. The scan is low contrast and the key is hard to read; use it with the 1909 sheet, which names the same workings in print.
- **Plate XCIII, "Sections illustrating the general geological structure of the country comprised in Plate XCI".** Cross-sections through the fold belt, which show that the anticlines are tight and near vertical. That is why the reefs are thin and steep, as the production figures in section 3.1 reflect.

### 9.6 How to read the overlays

- Switch an overlay on and slide its opacity down until the modern streets show through. The 1909 sheet is north-up and almost a plain plan, so streets and the Yarra bends should sit on their modern positions once the bounds are fitted. The 1893 sheet is also north-up but covers a far larger area at a smaller scale; use the Yarra loops at Pound Bend and Warrandyte as the check.
- On the 1909 sheet, follow a labelled anticline and look for the "xxxx" crosses: that is a line of old reef workings. Where that line meets a creek bed that is open to prospecting (section 5.2), the creek below it is the first place to pan.
- On the 1893 sheet, look for the yellow stipple on a creek: that is a recorded alluvial working. A solid black line is a quartz reef; a red line is a dyke.
- Neither sheet shows the park boundary or the three open creek sections. Keep the Parks Victoria figure (9.4) open beside the overlay before digging anywhere.

## 10. Field videos

Eight YouTube videos were examined. The first four (10.1 to 10.4) were transcribed from their auto-captions (names corrected) and read for claims. Three are at Warrandyte; one is a Ballarat detecting video included only as a comparison. Four more (10.5 to 10.8), added in round 3, are heritage and mine-exploration videos at Warrandyte, Diamond Creek and St Andrews. A fifth round 3 candidate, "Gold Hot Spots of Australia - Warrandyte, Victoria" (n95HuVdWBEQ), is no longer available on YouTube and is not included. Everything the presenters say is their own statement. Their rule statements were checked against the Parks Victoria pages and the 2006 prospecting figure, and they agree: prospecting in Warrandyte State Park is allowed only in the beds of Andersons, Stoney and Jumping Creeks; a Miner's Right is required; only non-mechanical tools (pan, simple cradle, hand sluice) are allowed and high bankers are not; holes must be backfilled; Andersons Creek is open only between the two orange markers near the Fourth Hill car park on Gold Memorial Road; Jumping Creek is open only upstream of the Jumping Creek Road bridge; Stoney Creek is open from the Yarra up to the Warrandyte-Research Road bridge and not above it [#56, #57, #323, #324, #399–#402, #410, #419, #420].

### 10.1 "Where to find gold in Warrandyte ?" — Murray Rivers Gold Adventures, 11 September 2025 (4PKH_sSOkFM, 12 min 49 s)

A guide to the three legal creeks. At Jumping Creek the presenter stands on the Jumping Creek Road bridge and says prospecting is allowed on the upstream side only; access is a walk past the guard rail and down a steep bank with no safe parking, and he calls it too dangerous for children; the gold is "not too bad" but hard to find [#410–#412]. At Andersons Creek he parks at the Fourth Hill car park on Gold Memorial Road, warns that the fire track must not be dug, and shows the orange marker about 10–20 m beyond each State Forest sign: only the creek bed between the two markers is open, and the creek outside them is "exempt" [#413–#417]. Andersons Creek gold is very fine with little black sand; he advises going at least 100 m from the car park [#416]. At Stoney Creek he describes the Yarra end, reached from Warrandyte-Research Road and The Boulevard, with the road bridge as the upper limit; the gold there has more heavies and black sand and suits beginners and children; old workings are visible on the creek sides [#418–#423]. He closes by saying the three creeks are for beginners, not for big returns [#424].

Rules stated: creek bed only; non-mechanical equipment only (pan, simple cradle or river sluice); no high bankers; do not dig the fire track; Andersons Creek only between the orange markers; Jumping Creek only upstream of the road bridge; Stoney Creek only up to the Warrandyte-Research Road bridge; do not block driveways when parking.

### 10.2 "Is there still chunky Gold left in Warrandyte ?" — Murray Rivers Gold Adventures, 1 July 2025 (uMheV8KFxC4, 19 min 53 s)

A sluicing session on Andersons Creek near the Fourth Hill car park in July, inferred from "Anderson's definitely flowing" and the fire-track remarks. He runs a 6-inch sluice with a rubber Dream Mat because mercury from the old batteries can wash out of other mats; he had found gold with mercury in clay at the spot before [#425, #426]. The first half bucket shows gold in the riffle; the decomposed bedrock has a contact layer and strongly mineralised quartz; the ground is solid clay with heavy ironstone gravel [#427–#430]. The first pan gives "reefy" gold of good size for Warrandyte, one piece silvered by mercury; a second run gives a few more bits and some lead; the total is about 0.03–0.07 (unit not spoken) [#429, #431, #432]. The creek rises quickly after rain and floods the dig within the session [#433]. His advice: gold drops where a bedrock bar pushes it against the bank, and on the outside bend as well as the inside; dig in the creek bed two or three shovels deep to clay, not into the banks, and fill the hole in [#434–#436].

Rules stated: creek bed only, not the banks; fill holes in; do not dig the fire track; do not undermine banks.

### 10.3 "Any gold in this crevice? Warrandyte" — Another Gold Prospector, 6 June 2024 (Ey3MNyWY-6M, 7 min 36 s)

Crevicing on Stoney Creek. The presenter takes about eight buckets from a pond-side crack for nothing, then pans the bottom of the crack with a yabby pump; other crevices give only specks, which he puts down to water moving too fast to hold gold [#437, #438]. The useful find is an abandoned tailings pile of clay-bound rock left by another digger: a pan from it shows gold because the clay was never broken up, so his advice is to process the clay [#439]. He objects to people deepening holes at spots shown in videos [#440]. The total for a few hours is about 0.16 (unit not spoken, probably grams) plus a bucket to process at home [#441]. He suspects someone has used a high banker on the creek and states that high bankers are not allowed in the three park creeks, only pans and cradles [#442].

Rules stated: no high bankers; pans and cradles only; do not deepen other people's holes.

### 10.4 "LIFE CHANGING 1kg GOLD NUGGET DISCOVERY!" — DIG IT DETECTING, 9 August 2024 (f8hgWV46KRo, 40 min 13 s) — outside the study area

This video is not in Warrandyte or Nillumbik. The video names no place; a Yahoo News Australia report (14 August 2024) puts the find in bushland at Ballarat, about 100 km west, in May 2024 [#443, #451]. It is included only as a comparison between metal detecting on a nugget field and panning creek gold at Warrandyte. The presenter detects with a Minelab GPX 6000 on a wet day, finds ten small nuggets within about an inch of the surface in red clay and pipe clay (under a gram in total), then digs a faint deep target about two pinpointers deep beside an old miner's hole and recovers an egg-sized nugget of 868 g (about 27.9 troy oz; the title's "1 kg" is rounded up) [#444–#450]. The contrast with Warrandyte is the point: the largest recorded Warrandyte nugget was about 100 oz in 1874 [#237, #384], and the modern creek finds in 10.1–10.3 are fractions of a gram. Detecting is not a useful method in the Warrandyte creeks, and it is not allowed off the creek beds in the park.

### 10.5 "Exploring Some Local GOLD History at Warrandyte" — Diggin' For Gold, 16 May 2018 (h22xaqweEj8, 14 min 41 s)

A walk through the Pound Bend tunnel (mouth about 4 by 6 m, about 145 m long) and The Island channel, then the Fourth Hill walk: grated side shafts and battery remains at the Victory, Johnson's 60 m adit, open cuts reworked by Chinese miners, and a 1950s hut that is now a pile of iron [#722–#728]. He ends at Anderson's Creek, where panning is allowed in a marked section, and gets a couple of specks from behind a rock bar after digging about a foot to clay [#729–#731]. Auto-captions mangle place names; the history statements match existing claims.

### 10.7 "Diamond Creek Gold Mines" — Unhinged Harry, 1 October 2019 (7QixO4q40dY, 14 min 40 s)

Two unnamed adits near Diamond Creek, filmed by phone light: one with a 30 ft left branch and visible caving, and one running west 80 to 100 ft to a north–south stope with inch-wide quartz veins; mullock heaps, cuttings and open shafts lie above [#733–#735].
